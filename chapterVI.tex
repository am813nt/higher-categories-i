    We have not shown yet that weak equivalences induce equivalences on mapping spaces if the target is a Kan complex:

    \subsection{Lemma}\label{chVI-1-1}
        Let $f \colon X \to Y$ be a weak equivalence of simplicial sets. Then for every Kan complex $K$, the natural map
        \begin{equation*}
            f^{*} \,\colon F(Y, K) \to F(X, K)
        \end{equation*}
        is a homotopy equivalence.
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}. By \ref{chV-3-11}, it suffices to check that $f^{*}$ induces a bijection on $\pi_0$ and isomorphisms on all $\pi_n$ for $n \geq 1$. The map is a bijection on $\pi_0$ by assumption. By the small object argument (\ref{chV-2-30}), there exists a Kan fibration $p \colon P \to K$ such tht $P$ is contractible. Let $G$ be the fibre of $p$ over any vertex of $K$. Then the long exact sequence of $p$ (\ref{chV-3-8}) implies that $\pi_{n+1}(F(X, K)) \cong \pi_n(F(X, G))$, so $f^{*}$ induces isomorphisms on $\pi_1$. Iterating this argument yields the analogous statement for all $n \geq 1$.\hfill$\Box$
        \medskip

        For quasicategories, we will try to develop a similar picture as far as it is possible to develop a parallel theory. Note that the mere possibility of defining analogous, but different concepts is very specific to the setting of simplicial sets. We originally decided, drawing on our intuition from topological spaces, to base our ``homotopy theory'' on lifting properties with respect to various cylinder inclusions. Then we observed that these lifting properties can equivalently be described in terms of horn inclusions. The theory of quasicategories is entirely based on horn inclusions; there is no description of quasicategories in terms of filling conditions for cylinders.\\
        One of the key points in simplicial homotopy theory was the fact that all notions behave very reasonably on simplicial sets for which the projection to $\Delta^0$ is a Kan fibration. Since quasicategories are defined by requiring the existence of inner horn fillers, there is a very natural notion of fibration to consider:

    \subsection{Definition}\label{chVI-1-2}
        Let $I H$ denote the set of \emph{inner horn inclusions}:
        \begin{equation*}
            I H \coloneq \{ \horn{n}{k} \to \Delta^n \mid 0 < k < n \} 
        \end{equation*}

    \subsection{Definition}\label{chVI-1-3}
        An \emph{inner fibration} is a map of simplicial sets which has the RLP with respect to $I H$.

    \subsection{Definition}\label{chVI-1-4}
        A map is \emph{inner anodyne} if it lies in $\sat(I H)$.

    \subsection{Corollary}\label{chVI-1-5}
        \begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*)]
            \item 
                A map is an inner fibration if and only if it has the RLP with respect to all inner anodyne maps.\label{chVI-1-5-1}
            \item 
                A map is inner anodyne if and only if it has the LLP with respect to all inner fibrations.\label{chVI-1-5-2}
        \end{enumerate}
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}. \ref{chVI-1-5-1} is clear from \ref{chV-2-2}. Every inner anodyne map has the LLP with respect to inner fibrations by \ref{chV-2-2}, and the converse of \ref{chVI-1-5-2} is provided by \ref{chV-2-31}.\hfill$\Box$
        \medskip

        Despite our best efforts to come up with a notion of cofibration which is analogous to the three different notions of fibration we had before (left, right and Kan), we ended up with the same class of morphisms, namely the injections. Since we do not have any better idea what we should do now (and since homotopy extension properties do not make any sense in the absence of cylinders), we will stick to this notion of cofibration.\\
        Defining a notion of weak equivalence was already a somewhat subtle point in the previous chapter. For maps between quasicategories, we discussed a notion of equivalence in Chapter IV; at the moment, there is no reasonable guess what a notion of “categorical equivalence” might be for arbitrary simplicial sets.

    \subsection{Definition}\label{chVI-1-6}
        A functor $F \colon \Cc \to \Dd$ is a \emph{Joyal equivalence} if there exist a functor $G \colon \Dd \to \Cc$ and natural equivalences $GF \sim \id_{\Cc}$ and $FG \sim \id_{\Dd}$.

    \subsection{Remark}\label{chVI-1-7}
        Note that, the way we have defined natural equivalences, there is no ambiguity about the directionality of the natural equivalences $GF \sim \id_{\Cc}$ and $FG \sim \id_{\Dd}$ since natural equivalences can always be inverted.

\section{Inner anodyne maps}\label{chVI-2}
    As with anodyne maps, we need a better understanding of the class of inner anodynes to get things going. In the following, we use the shorthand notation $S \boxtimes T$ for two classes of morphisms $S$ and $T$ to mean
    \begin{equation*}
        S \boxtimes T \coloneq \{ s \boxtimes t \mid s \in S, \; t \in T \} \,.
    \end{equation*}

    \subsection{Lemma}\label{chVI-2-1}
        Every inner horn inclusion $\horn{n}{k} \to \Delta^n$ (i.e. $0 < k <n$) is a retract of $(\horn{n}{k} \to \Delta^n) \boxtimes (\horn{2}{1} \to \Delta^2)$.
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}. Define maps of posets
        \begin{align*}
            s \,\colon [n] &\to [n] \times [2] \\
            i &\mapsto
            \begin{cases}
                (i, 0) & i < k \\
                (i, 1) & i = k \\
                (i, 2) & i > k
            \end{cases}
        \end{align*}
        and
        \begin{align*}
            r \,\colon [n] \times [2] &\to [n]\\
            (i, j) &\mapsto
            \begin{cases}
                i & i < k, \, j = 0 \\
                i & i > k, \, j = 2 \\
                k & \text{else}
            \end{cases}
        \end{align*}
        Evidently, $rs = \id_{[n]}$. We need to check that $s$ and $r$ restrict appropriately on nerves. Observer that a simplex $\alpha \colon [p] \to [n]$ lies in $\horn{n}{k}$ if and only if $[n] \setminus \{k\} \nsubseteq \im \alpha$.\\
        Note that the composition of $s$ with the projection $\pr \colon [n] \times [2] \to [n]$ onto the first factor is also the identity. Hence, $\pr s \alpha$ lies in $\horn{n}{k}$, so $s \alpha$ is in $\horn{n}{k} \times \Delta^2$.\\
        Let $\alpha \colon [p] \to [n] \times [2]$ represent a simplex in $\horn{n}{k} \times \Delta^2$, i.e. $[n] \setminus \{k\} \nsubseteq \im (\pr \alpha)$. Then there exists $i \ne k$ such that $i \notin \im(\pr \alpha)$. Since $i \ne k$, the preimage of $i$ under $r$ consists of precisely one point, namely either $(i, 0)$ for $i < k$ or $(i, 2)$ for $i > k$. Since $i \notin \im(\pr \alpha)$, we have $\im(\alpha) \cap r^{-1}(i) = \varnothing$ and thus $i \notin \im(r \alpha)$. Hence $r \alpha$ defines a simplex in $\horn{n}{k}$.\\
        If $\alpha \colon [p] \to [n] \times [2]$ represents s simplex in $\Delta^n \times \horn{2}{1}$, then there exists $j \in \{0, 2\}$ such that $j \notin \im(\pr' \alpha)$, where $\pr' \colon [n] \times [2] \to [2]$ is the projection onto the second factor. Then it follows from the definition of $r$ that $\im(r \alpha)$ contains either no $i < k$ (if $j = 0$) or no $i > k$ (if $j = 1$). Since $0 < k < n$, both the sets $\{ i \mid 0 \leq i < k \}$ and $\{ i \mid k < i \leq n \}$ are non-empty, so there exists some $i \ne k$ with $i \notin \im(r \alpha)$.\hfill$\Box$

    \subsection{Proposition}\label{chVI-2-2}
        The following classes of morphisms generate the same saturated class:
        \begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*)]
            \item 
                $I H$;
            \item 
                $B \boxtimes \{ \horn{2}{1} \to \Delta^2 \}$;
            \item 
                $I \boxtimes \{ \horn{2}{1} \to \Delta^2 \}$;
            \item 
                $I \boxtimes I H$.
        \end{enumerate}
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}. Obviously,
        \begin{equation*}
            \sat(B \boxtimes \{ \horn{2}{1} \to \Delta^2 \}) \subseteq \sat(I \boxtimes \{ \horn{2}{1} \to \Delta^2 \}) \subseteq \sat(I \boxtimes I H) \,.
        \end{equation*}
        The inclusion
        \begin{equation*}
            I \boxtimes \{ \horn{2}{1} \to \Delta^2 \} \subseteq \sat(B \boxtimes \{ \horn{2}{1} \to \Delta^2 \})
        \end{equation*}
        follows as in \ref{chV-2-9} by considering the relative skeletal filtration of an injection.\\
        Let $i$ be an arbitrary injection. By \ref{chVI-2-1}, the map $i \boxtimes (\horn{n}{k} \to \Delta^n)$ is a retract of
        \begin{equation*}
            i \boxtimes ((\horn{n}{k} \to \Delta^n) \boxtimes (\horn{2}{1} \to \Delta^2)) \cong (i \boxtimes (\horn{n}{k} \to \Delta^n)) \boxtimes (\horn{2}{1} \to \Delta^2) \,.
        \end{equation*}
        Since $i \boxtimes (\horn{n}{k} \to \Delta^n)$ is an injection, this shows
        \begin{equation*}
            I \boxtimes I H \subseteq \sat(I \boxtimes (\horn{2}{1} \to \Delta^2)) \,.
        \end{equation*}
        Also by \ref{chVI-2-1}, $I H$ is contained in the saturation of $I \boxtimes \{\horn{2}{1} \to \Delta^2\}$.\\
        So the only thing left to show is that $(\partial \Delta^n \to \Delta^n) \boxtimes (\horn{2}{1} \to \Delta^2)$ is contained in the saturation of $I H$. We do this by explicit horn-filling.\\
        For $n = 0$, note that
        \begin{equation*}
            (\partial \Delta^n \to \Delta^n) \boxtimes (\horn{2}{1} \to \Delta^2) \cong (\horn{2}{1} \to \Delta^2) \,,
        \end{equation*}
        so we may assume $n > 0$.\\
        The simplicial set $\Delta^n \times \Delta^2$ is the nerve of the poset $[n] \times [2]$, so non-degenerate simplices are given by injective maps $[p] \to [n] \times [2]$. For $p < n$, the composition of any map $\alpha \colon [n] \times [2]$ with the projection $\pr \colon [n] \times [2] \to [n]$ cannot be surjective, so such a simplex is contained in $\partial \Delta^n \times \Delta^2$. In particular, $(\partial \Delta^n \times \Delta^2) \underset{\partial \Delta^n \times \horn{2}{1}}{\cup} (\Delta^n \times \horn{2}{1})$ is only missing $n$-simplices, $(n+1)$-simplices and $(n+2)$-simplices. Moreover, all $(n+2)$-simplices are missing.\\
        Any injective map $[n + 2] \to [n] \times [2]$ is uniquely determined by the two indices at which its value jumps in the second coordinate. Hence we can enumerate all non-degenerate $(n + 2)$-simplices in $\Delta^n \times \Delta^2$ as $(\tau_{i,j})_{0 \leq i \leq j \leq n}$, where we set
        \begin{equation*}
            \tau_{i j}(k) \coloneq
            \begin{cases}
                (k, 0) & 0 \leq k \leq i \\
                (k - 1, 1) & i + 1 \leq k \leq j + 1 \\
                (k - 2, 2) & j + 2 \leq k \leq n + 2
            \end{cases}
        \end{equation*}
        To glue these simplices in, we need to understand their face structure:
        \begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*)]
            \item 
                For $l \notin \{i, i + 1, j + 1, j + 2\}$, $\pr \circ d_l \tau_{i j}$ is not surjective, So $d_l \tau_{i j} \in \partial \Delta^n \times \Delta^2$.\label{chVI-2-2-1}
            \item 
                The simplex $d_{j+2} \tau_{i j}$ is given by
                \begin{equation*}
                    k \mapsto
                    \begin{cases}
                        (k, 0) & 0 \leq k \leq i \\
                        (k - 1, 1) & i + 1 \leq k \leq j + 1 \\
                        (k - 1, 2) & j + 2 \leq k \leq n + 1
                    \end{cases}
                \end{equation*}
                Call this simplex $\sigma_{i j}$. It is defined for $0 \leq i \leq j < n$, and it is a non-degenerate $(n+1)$-simplex in $\Delta^n \times \Delta^2$. Since both $\pr \circ \sigma_{i j}$ and $\pr' \circ \sigma_{i j}$ are surjective, where $\pr' \colon [n] \times [2] \to [2]$ is the projection onto the second coordinate, none of these simplices is contained in $\partial \Delta^n \times \Delta^2 \underset{\partial \Delta^n \times \horn{2}{1}}{\cup} (\Delta^n \times \horn{2}{1})$.\label{chVI-2-2-2}
            \item 
                For $i < j$, the simplex $d_{j+1} \tau_{i j}$ is given by
                \begin{equation*}
                    k \mapsto
                    \begin{cases}
                        (k, 0) & 0 \leq k \leq i \\
                        (k - 1, 1) & i + 1 \leq k \leq j \\
                        (k - 1, 2) & j + 1 \leq k \leq n + 1
                    \end{cases}
                \end{equation*}
                So $d_{j+1} \tau_{i j} = \sigma_{i, j-1}$.\label{chVI-2-2-3}
            \item 
                If $i = 0$, then $0 \notin \pr' \circ d_i \tau_{i j}$, so $d_i \tau_{i j} \in \Delta^n \times \horn{2}{1}$.\label{chVI-2-2-4}
            \item 
                If $i > 0$, then $d_i \tau_{i j}$ is given by
                \begin{equation*}
                    k \mapsto
                    \begin{cases}
                        (k, 0) & 0 \leq k < i \\
                        (k, 1) & i \leq k \leq j \\
                        (k - 1, 2) & j + 1 \leq k \leq n + 1
                    \end{cases}
                \end{equation*}
                so $d_i \tau_{i j} = d_i \tau_{i-1, j}$.\label{chVI-2-2-5}
        \end{enumerate}
        The remaining face $d_{i+1} \tau_{i j}$ is given by
        \begin{equation*}
            k \mapsto
            \begin{cases}
                (k, 0) & 0 \leq k \leq i \\
                (k, 1) & i + 1 \leq k \leq j \\
                (k - 1, 2) & j + 1 \leq k \leq n + 1
            \end{cases}
        \end{equation*}
        This entails:
        \begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*)]
            \setcounter{enumi}{5}
            \item 
                For $k < i$, $d_{i+1} \tau_{i j}$ is not a face of $\tau_{k j}$ for any $k < i$: this holds since $(i, 0) \in \im(d_{i+1} \tau_{ij})$, but $(i, 0) \notin \im(\tau_{k j})$.\label{chVI-2-2-6}
            \item 
                For $l < j$, $d_{i+1} \tau_{i j}$ is not a face of $\tau_{k l}$: this holds since $(j, 1) \in \im(d_{i+1} \tau_{i j})$, but $(j, 1) \notin \im(\tau_{k l})$.\label{chVI-2-2-7}
        \end{enumerate}
        The collection of $(n+1)$-simplices $(\sigma_{i j})_{0 \leq i \leq j < n}$ naturally appeared as faces of the $\tau_{i j}$, so let us understand their face structure next:
        \begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*)]
            \setcounter{enumi}{7}  
            \item 
                For $l \notin \{ i, i + 1 \}$, $\pr \circ d_l \sigma_{i j}$ is not surjective, so $d_l \sigma_{i j} \in \partial \Delta^n \times \Delta^2$.\label{chVI-2-2-8}
            \item 
                If $i = 0$, then $0 \notin \im(\pr \circ d_i \sigma_{i j})$, so $d_0 \sigma_{0 j} \in \partial \Delta^n \times \Delta^2$.\label{chVI-2-2-9}
            \item 
                If $i > 0$, the face $d_i \sigma_{i j}$ is given by
                \begin{equation*}
                    k \mapsto
                    \begin{cases}
                        (k, 0) & 0 \leq k < i \\
                        (k, 1) & i \leq k \leq j \\
                        (k, 2) & j + 1 \leq k \leq n
                    \end{cases}
                \end{equation*}
                so $d_i \sigma_{i j} = d_i \sigma_{i-1, j}$.\label{chVI-2-2-10}  
        \end{enumerate}
        Once more, the interesting remaining face is $d_{i+1} \sigma_{i j}$. This face is given by
        \begin{equation*}
            k \mapsto
            \begin{cases}
                (k, 0) & 0 \leq k \leq i \\
                (k, 1) & i + 1 \leq k \leq j \\
                (k, 2) & j + 1 \leq k \leq n
            \end{cases}
        \end{equation*}
        Observe the following:
        \begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*)]
            \setcounter{enumi}{10}
            \item 
                $d_{i+1} \sigma_{i j} \notin (\partial \Delta^n \times \Delta^2) \underset{\partial \Delta^n \times \horn{2}{1}}{\cup} (\Delta^n \times \horn{2}{1})$: this holds since $\{ 0, 2 \} \subseteq \im(\pr' \circ d_{i+1} \sigma_{i j})$ and $\pr \circ d_{i+1} \sigma_{i j}$ is surjective.\label{chVI-2-2-11}
            \item 
                For $k < i$ and $l \leq j$, $d_{i+1} \sigma_{i j}$ is not a face of $\sigma_{k l}$: this holds since $(i, 0) \in \im(d_{i+1} \sigma_{i j})$ and $(i, 0) \notin \im(\sigma_{k l})$.\label{chVI-2-2-12}
            \item 
                For $l < j$, $d_{i+1} \sigma_{i j}$ is not a face of $\sigma_{k l}$: if $i < j$, then $(j, 1) \in \im(d_{i+1} \sigma_{i j})$, but $(j, 1) \notin \im(\sigma_{k l})$. If $i = j$, then $k \leq l < j = i$, so \ref{chVI-2-2-12} applies.\label{chVI-2-2-13} 
        \end{enumerate}
        Now we only need to assemble the information we have gathered. Define $X(0, 0) \coloneq (\partial \Delta^n \times \Delta^2) \underset{\partial \Delta^n}{\cup} (\Delta^n \times \horn{2}{1})$. Proceeding by induction, we build simplicial subsets of $\Delta^n \times \Delta^2$ by letting $X(i + 1, j)$ be given by the pushout
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \horn{n+1}{i+1} \arrow[d] \arrow[r, "{\sigma_{i j}|}_{\ldots}"] & X(i, j) \arrow[d] \\
                \Delta^{n+1} \arrow[r, "\sigma_{i j}"] & X(i + 1, j)
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        for $0 \leq i \leq j < n$ and setting $X(0, j + 1) \coloneq X(j + 1, j)$. We have to check that the pushout squares above actually exist as claimed:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item 
                For the start of induction, \ref{chVI-2-2-8} and \ref{chVI-2-2-9} imply that $\sigma_{0 0}$ induces a map $\horn{n+1}{1} \to X(0, 0)$. \ref{chVI-2-2-11} shows that $\sigma_{0 0}$ induces an injective map $X(1, 0) \to \Delta^n \times \Delta^2$.
            \item 
                For $i = 0$ and $j \geq 1$, \ref{chVI-2-2-8} and \ref{chVI-2-2-9} imply that $\sigma_{0 j}$ induces a map $\horn{n+1}{1} \to X(0, j)$. \ref{chVI-2-2-11} and \ref{chVI-2-2-13} show that $\sigma_{0 j}$ induces an injective map $X(1, j) \to \Delta^n \times \Delta^2$.
            \item 
                For $i \geq 1$ and $j \geq 1$, \ref{chVI-2-2-8} and \ref{chVI-2-2-10} imply that $\sigma_{i j}$ induces a map $\horn{n+1}{1} \to X(i, j)$. \ref{chVI-2-2-11}, \ref{chVI-2-2-12} and \ref{chVI-2-2-13} show that $\sigma_{i j}$ induces an injective map $X(i + 1, j) \to \Delta^n \times \Delta^2$.
        \end{itemize}
        Observe that $X(i, j) \to X(i + 1, j)$ is inner anodyne. Thus, so is $X(0, j) \to X(0, j + 1)$ and therefore $X(0, 0) \to X(0, n)$. Set $Y(0, 0) \coloneq X(0, n)$.\\
        Then we do a second induction to define for $i \leq j \leq n$ the simplicial subset $Y(i + 1, j)$ by a pushout
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \horn{n+2}{i+1} \arrow[d] \arrow[r, "{\tau_{i j}|}_{\ldots}"] & Y(i, j) \arrow[d] \\
                \Delta^{n+2} \arrow[r, "\tau_{i j}"] & Y(i + 1, j)
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        and by defining $Y(0, j + 1) \coloneq Y(j + 1, j)$. Again, we have to check that the pushout squares above actually exist as claimed:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item 
                For the start of the induction, \ref{chVI-2-2-1}, \ref{chVI-2-2-2} and \ref{chVI-2-2-4} show that $\tau_{0 0}$ induces a map $\horn{n+1}{i+1} \to Y(0, 0)$. By the formula for $d_1 \tau_{0 0}$, one observes that $\im(d_1 \tau_{0 0}) = \{0, 2\}$, and none of the $(n+1)$-simplices in $Y(0, 0)$ has this property. So the pushout $Y(1, 0)$ is in fact a simplicial subset of $\Delta^n \times \Delta^2$.
            \item 
                For $i = 0$ and $j \geq 1$, \ref{chVI-2-2-1}, \ref{chVI-2-2-2}, \ref{chVI-2-2-3} and \ref{chVI-2-2-4} imply that $\tau_{i j}$ induces a map $\horn{n+1}{i+1} \to Y(0, j)$. The remaining face $d_1 \tau_{0 j}$ is not of the form $\sigma_{k l}$, and by \ref{chVI-2-2-6} and \ref{chVI-2-2-7} it is also not a face if any of the preceding $\tau_{k l}$. So $Y(1, j)$ is a simplicial subset of $\Delta^n \times \Delta^2$.
            \item 
                For $i, j \geq 1$, \ref{chVI-2-2-1}, \ref{chVI-2-2-2}, \ref{chVI-2-2-3} and \ref{chVI-2-2-5} imply that $\tau_{i j}$ induces a map $\horn{n+1}{i+1} \to Y(i, j)$. The remaining face $d_{i+1} \tau_{i j}$ is not of the form $\sigma_{k l}$, and by \ref{chVI-2-2-6} and \ref{chVI-2-2-7} it is also not a face of any of the preceding $\tau_{k l}$. So $Y(i + 1, j)$ is a simplicial subset of $\Delta^n \times \Delta^2$.
        \end{itemize}
        All the inclusions $Y(i, j) \to Y(i + 1, j)$ are inner anodyne by definition, and therefore so is $Y(0, 0) \to Y(0, n + 1)$. Since $Y(0, n + 1)$ contains all non-degenerate $(n + 2)$-simplices of $\Delta^n \times \Delta^2$, we have $Y(0, n + 1) = \Delta^n \times \Delta^2$. Consequently, the inclusion $(\partial \Delta^n \times \Delta^2) \underset{\partial \delta^n \times \horn{2}{1}}{\cup} (\Delta^n \times \horn{2}{1}) = X(0, 0) \to X(0, n) = Y(0, 0) \to Y(0, n + 1) = \Delta^n \times \Delta^2$ is inner anodyne as claimed.\hfill$\Box$

    \subsection{Corollary}\label{chVI-2-3}
        Let $i$ be an inner anodyne morphism and $j$ a cofibration. Then $i \boxtimes j$ is inner anodyne.
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}. The class
        \begin{equation*}
            \Sigma \coloneq \{ i \mid i \boxtimes j \text{ inner anodyne} \}
        \end{equation*}
        is saturated by Exercise 3 on Sheet 7. Hence, it suffices to check that $I H \subseteq \Sigma$. This is true by \ref{chVI-2-2}.\hfill$\Box$

    \subsection{Corollary}\label{chVI-2-4}
        Let $i \colon A \to B$ be a cofibration and let $p \colon X \to Y$ be an inner fibration. Then induced map
        \begin{equation*}
            F(B, X) \to F(A, X) \underset{F(A, Y)}{\times} F(B, Y)
        \end{equation*}
        is an inner fibration. If $i$ is inner anodyne, this map is a trivial Kan fibration.
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}. As usual, every lifting problem
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                U \arrow[d, "j"'] \arrow[r] & F(B, X) \arrow[d] \\
                V \arrow[r] & F(A, X) \underset{F(A, Y)}{\times} F(B, Y)
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        translates to a lifting problem
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                (U \times B) \underset{U \times A}{\cup} (V \times A) \arrow[d, "i \,\boxtimes\, j"'] \arrow[r] & X \arrow[d] \\
                V \times B \arrow[r] & Y
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        If $i$ or $j$ is inner anodyne, then $i \boxtimes j$ is inner anodyne by \ref{chVI-2-3}. This implies the corollary.\hfill$\Box$

    \subsection{Corollary}\label{chVI-2-5}
        A simplicial set $\Cc$ is a quasicategory if and only if the restriction map $F(\Delta^2, \Cc) \to F(\horn{2}{1}, \Cc)$ is a trivial Kan fibration.
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}. Suppose that $\Cc$ is a quasicategory, i.e. $\Cc \to \Delta^0$ is an inner fibration. Since $\horn{2}{1} \to \Delta^2$ is inner anodyne, it follows from \ref{chVI-2-4} that $F(\Delta^2, \Cc) \to F(\horn{2}{1}, \Cc)$ is a trivial Kan fibration.\\
        Conversely, \ref{chVI-2-2} implies that it suffices to show that $\Cc \to \Delta^0$ has the RLP with respect to $I \boxtimes \{\horn{2}{1} \to \Delta^2\}$. Let $i \colon A \to B$ be a cofibration. Since every lifting problem
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                (A \times \Delta^2) \underset{A \times \horn{2}{1}}{\cup} (B \times \horn{2}{1}) \arrow[d, "{i \,\boxtimes\, (\horn{2}{1} \to \Delta^2)}"'] \arrow[r] & \Cc \arrow[d] \\
                B \times \Delta^2 \arrow[r] & \Delta^0
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        corresponds to a lifting problem
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                A \arrow[d, "i"'] \arrow[r] & F(\Delta^2, \Cc) \arrow[d] \\
                B \arrow[r] & F(\horn{2}{1}, \Cc)
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        a solution exists by assumption.\hfill$\Box$

    \subsection{Corollary (Theorem B)}\label{chVI-2-6}
        Let $f \colon y \to z$ and $g \colon x \to y$ be edges in a quasicategory $\Cc$. Then $\comp(f, g) \coloneq \Delta^0 \underset{F(\horn{2}{1}, \Cc)}{\times} F(\Delta^2, \Cc)$ is a contractible Kan complex.
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}. This is immediate from \ref{chVI-2-4} and \ref{chV-3-18}.\hfill$\Box$

    \subsection{Remark}\label{chVI-2-7}
        \ref{chVI-2-4} also implies that the ``mapping space''
        \begin{equation*}
            \Hom_{\Cc}(x, y) = \Delta^0 \underset{\Cc \times \Cc}{\times} F(\Delta^1, \Cc)
        \end{equation*}
        between any two objects $x, y$ in a quasicategory $\Cc$ is itself a quasicategory (because $\partial \Delta^1 \to \Delta^1$ is a cofibration). Of course, this is still quite a bit away from the statement of \hyperref[chIII-4]{Theorem A} (claiming that it is a Kan complex).

    \subsection{Corollary [Theorem D, first part]}\label{chVI-2-8}
        If $\Cc$ is a quasicategory and $X$ is a simplicial set, then $F(X, \Cc)$ is also a quasicategory.
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}. Apply \ref{chVI-2-4} with $i = \varnothing \to X$ and $p \colon \Cc \to \Delta^0$.\hfill$\Box$
        \medskip

        As a consequence of \ref{chVI-2-8}, we will prefer to write $\Fun(X, \Cc)$ instead of $F(X, \Cc)$ if $\Cc$ is a quasicategory.\\
        \ref{chVI-2-8} puts us in a position where we can finally define what a ``categorical equivalence'' between arbitrary simplicial sets is.

    \subsection{Definition}\label{chVI-2-9}
        A map $f \colon X \to Y$ of simplicial sets is a \emph{Joyal equivalence} (or \emph{categorical equivalence}) if the restriction map
        \begin{equation*}
            \pi f^{*} \,\colon \pi \Fun(Y, \Cc) \to \pi \Fun(X, \Cc)
        \end{equation*}
        is an equivalence of categories for every quasicategory $\Cc$.

    \subsection{Corollary}\label{chVI-2-10}
        A map of quasicategories is a Joyal equivalence in the sense of \ref{chVI-1-6} if and only if it is a Joyal equivalence in the sense of \ref{chVI-2-9}.
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}. If $F \colon \Cc \to \Dd$ admits an inverse $G \colon \Dd \to \Cc$ witnessed by natural equivalences $GF \sim \id_{\Cc}$ and $FG \sim \id_{\Dd}$, then $F^{*} \colon \pi \Fun(\Dd, \Ee) \to \pi \Fun(\Cc, \Ee)$ has an evident inverse, namely $G^{*}$.\\
        Conversely, the essential surjectivity of $\pi F^{*} \colon \pi \Fun(\Dd, \Cc) \to \pi \Fun(\Cc, \Cc)$ yields a functor $G \colon \Dd \to \Cc$ such that $GF \sim \id_{\Cc}$. Then $\pi G^{*} \colon \pi \Fun(\Cc, \Dd) \to \pi \Fun(\Dd, \Dd)$ is also an equivalence, so there exists $F' \colon \Cc \to \Dd$ such that $F'G \sim \id_{\Dd}$. It follows that $FG \sim F'GFG \sim F'G \sim \id_{\Dd}$.

    \subsection{Example}\label{chVI-2-11}
        A functor $F \colon \Cc \to \Dd$ of categories is an equivalence if and only if $N F$ is a Joyal equivalence.

    \subsection{Lemma}\label{chVI-2-12}
        \begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*)]
            \item 
                A map of Kan complexes is a Joyal equivalence if and only if it  is a homotopy equivalence.\label{chVI-2-12-1}
            \item 
                Every trivial Kan fibration is a Joyal equivalence.\label{chVI-2-12-2}
            \item 
                every inner anodyne map is a Joyal equivalence.\label{chVI-2-12-3}
            \item 
                Every Joyal equivalence is a weak equivalence.\label{chVI-2-12-4}
        \end{enumerate}
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}. Any Joyal equivalence of quasicategories is a particular kind of simplicial homotopy equivalence. Conversely, if $f \colon X \to Y$ is a homotopy equivalence, we find $g \colon Y \to X$ and homotopies $h \colon gf \simeq \id_X$ and $k \colon gf \simeq \id_Y$. Since $F(X, X)$ and $F(Y, Y)$ are Kan complexes, there are also homotopies $h' \colon \id_X \simeq gf$ and $k' \colon \id_Y \simeq gf$ such that $h$ and $h'$ compose to the constant homotopy on $gf$, and $k$ and $k'$ compose to the constant homotopy on $\id_Y$ (compositions are taken in $F(X, X)$ and $F(Y, Y)$, respectively). The diagram
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                & gf \arrow[dr, "h"] \arrow[rr, "{s(gf)}"] & & gf \\
                \id_X \arrow[ur, "h'"] \arrow[urrr, dotted] \arrow[rr, "{s(\id_X)}"] & & \id_X \arrow[ur, "h'"] &
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        in $F(X, X)$ defines a right horn: the bottom face and left face are degenerate with the dotted arrow unlabeled, the right face is the one arising from the definition of $h'$, and the front face is missing. Filling this horn shows that $h'$ and $h$ also compose to the constant homotopy on $\id_X$. The argument for $k$ and $k'$ is analogous.\\
        Let $p \colon X \to Y$ be a trivial Kan fibration. Choose a section $s \colon Y \to X$. Then the lifting problem
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                X \times \Delta^{\{0,2\}} \sqcup X \times \Delta^{\{1,3\}} \arrow[d] \arrow[r, "\pr \sqcup \pr"] & X \sqcup X \arrow[r, "\id_X \sqcup \, sp"] & X \arrow[d, "p"] \\
                X \times \Delta^3 \arrow[r, "\pr"] & X \arrow[r, "p"] & Y
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        has a solution. Restricting to along the spine inclusion $X \times I_3 \to X \times \Delta^3$ yields three natural transformations $\tau_1 \colon \id_X \Rightarrow s p$, $\tau_2 \colon s p \Rightarrow \id_X$, $\tau_3 \colon \id_X \Rightarrow s p$ such that $\id_X$ is a composition of both $\tau_2$ and $\tau_1$ as well as $\tau_3$ and $\tau_2$ (in $\Fun(X, X)$). Since equivalences in a quasicategory satisfy two-out-of-six (by \ref{chIII-8}), it follows that $s p$ and $\id_X$ are naturally equivalent.\\
        Let $i \colon A \to B$ be inner anodyne. By \ref{chVI-2-4}, the restriction map
        \begin{equation*}
            i^{*} \,\colon \Fun(B, \Cc) \to \Fun(A, \Cc)
        \end{equation*}
        is a trivial Kan fibration. The first part of the lemma shows that $i^{*}$ is a Joyal equivalence, so it follows that $\pi i^{*}$ is an equivalence of categories.\\
        Let $f \colon X \to Y$ be a Joyal equivalence. Since Kan complexes are quasicategories, $f$ induces for every Kan complex $K$ an equivalence of categories
        \begin{equation*}
            \pi f^{*} \,\colon \pi F(Y, K) \to \pi F(X, K) \,.
        \end{equation*}
        Equivalences of categories induce bijections on the sets of connected components, so $\pi_0 f^{*}$ is a bijection.\hfill$\Box$

    \subsection{Remark}\label{chVI-2-13}
        It may seem pedantic that we gave proofs for \ref{chVI-2-12} \ref{chVI-2-12-2} and \ref{chVI-2-12-1}, but here is an example to illustrate that this is necessary: Suppose that $\Cc$ and $\Dd$ are Kan-enriched categories, and that we have functors $F \colon \Cc \to \Dd$ as well as $G \colon \Dd \to \Cc$ together with natural transformations $u \colon \id_{\Cc} \to GF$ and $c \colon FG \to \id_{\Dd}$ such that $u_x$ is a weak equivalence for every $x \in \Cc$ and $c_y$ is a weak equivalence for every $y \in \Dd$; the adjunction between $\gr{-} \colon \Kan \to \CW$ and $\Sing \colon \CW \to \Kan$ is an example of this situation.\\
        Applying the coherent nerve, we obtain functors $N^c F, N^c G$ together with natural transformations $\id \to N^c G N^c F$ and $N^c F N^c G \to \id$ through equivalences. But this is not yet enough to conclude that $N^c F$ is a Joyal equivalence because we cannot say anything at the moment about the invertibility of the natural transformations. This would either rely on \hyperref[chIII-thm-c]{Theorem E}, which we have not proved yet, or we would have to provide inverse transformation on the Kan-enriched level.

\section{Joins and Slices}\label{chVI-3}
    The next result from the list announced in Chapter III that we a going to prove is \hyperref[chIII-thm-c]{Theorem C}, but the proof requires some preparations. It relies on the formalism of \emph{slices}. To motivate their definition, consider the following situation: let $\Cc$ be a category and $f \colon x \to y$ a morphism in $\Cc$. Then one observes that $f$ is an isomorphism in $\Cc$ if and only if the forgetful functor
    \begin{align*}
        \Cc_{f/} \coloneq \{ \text{cones under } f \} &\to \{ \text{cones under } x \} = x / \Cc \\
        \begin{tikzcd}[sep=tiny,ampersand replacement=\&]
            x \arrow[dd, "f"'] \arrow[dr] \& \\
            \& c \\
            y \arrow[ur] \&
        \end{tikzcd}
        &\mapsto (x \to x)
    \end{align*}
    is an isomorphism of categories. The ``only if'' part is clear. If $\Cc_{f/} \to x / \Cc$ is an isomorphism, then $x \xrightarrow{\id_x} x$ has a preimage, which is a morphism $g \colon y \to x$ such that $gf = \id_x$. Since
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{tikzcd}[sep=small]
            & y \\
            x \arrow[ur, "f"] \arrow[dr, "\id_x"'] \\
            & x \arrow[uu, "f"']
        \end{tikzcd}
    \end{equation*}
    is a morphism in $x / \Cc$, the pair $(\id_x, g)$ is a preimage of the dmain and $(f, \id_y)$ is a preimage of the target, it follows that $fg = \id_y$.\\
    It will turn out that a similar statement holds for quasicategories, namely that a morphism $f$ in a quasicategory is an equivalence if and only if a certain forgetful functor $\Cc_{f/} \to \Cc_{x/}$ is a trivial Kan fibration. It will then only be a matter of decoding to see that certain outer horn fillers exist. Note that we have not yet made sense of the symbols, but they a supposed to indicate categories or appropriate cones.\\
    In the 1-categorical situation, the following construction formalises the concept of cones:

    \subsection{Definition}\label{chVI-3-1}
        Let $\Cc$ and $\Dd$ be ordinary categories. Define their \emph{join} $\Cc \star \Dd$ as the category whose objects are $\obj \Cc \sqcup \obj \Dd$ and whose morphisms are given by
        \begin{equation*}
            \Hom_{\Cc \star \Dd}(x, y) \coloneq
            \begin{cases}
                \Hom_{\Cc}(x, y) & x, y \in \Cc \\
                \Hom_{\Dd}(x, y) & x, y \in \Dd \\
                \{*\} & x \in \Cc, y \in \Dd \\
                \varnothing & x \in \Dd, y \in \Cc \,,
            \end{cases}
        \end{equation*}
        together with the obvious composition rules.
        \medskip

        So the join connects each object in $\Cc$ with each object in $\Dd$ via unique morphism. Now note that objects in $\Cc_{f/}$ are given by certain diagrams $[1] \star \{c\} \to \Cc$, and morphisms in $\Cc_{f/}$ correspond to certain functors $[1] \star \{c \leq c'\} \to \Cc$. This suggests to define an $n$-simplex in the quasicategory $\Cc_{f/}$ to be a certain diagram $\Delta^1 \star \Delta^n \to \Cc$. Of course, this is in no way specific to the situation of cones under a morphism, and $\Delta^1$ can be replaced by any diagram shape. This leads us to consider joins of arbitrary simplicial sets.

    \subsection{Definition}\label{chVI-3-2}
        Define the functor cut as the representable functor
        \begin{equation*}
            \cut \coloneq \Hom_{\LinOrd}(-, [1]) \,\colon \LinOrd^{\op} \to \Set
        \end{equation*}
        Note that for $L \in \LinOrd$, there is a natural bijection
        \begin{equation*}
            \Hom_{\LinOrd}(L, [1]) \cong \{(L_0, L_1) \mid L_0 \cup L_1 = L,\, L_0 \cap L_1 = \varnothing,\, \forall l_0 \in L_0 \; \forall l_1 \in L_1 : l_0 < l_1 \} \,,
        \end{equation*}
        which explains the name of the functor.

    \subsection{Definition}\label{chVI-3-3}
        Let $c \colon [n] \to [1]$ be a cut in $[n]$. Define for $i \in \{0, 1\}$ the simplicial set $\Delta_{c,i}$ as the pullback
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \Delta_{c,i} \arrow[d] \arrow[r] & \Delta^n \arrow[d, "c"] \\
                \{i\} \arrow[r] & \Delta^1
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        Observe that $\Delta_{c,i}$ is isomorphic to a standard simplex.

    \subsection{Definition}\label{chVI-3-4}
        Let $X$ and $Y$ be simplicial sets. Define their \emph{join} as follows: for $n \geq 0$, set
        \begin{equation*}
            (X \star Y)_n \coloneq \coprod\limits_{c \in \cut[n]} \Hom_{\sSet}(\Delta_{c,0}, X) \times \Hom_{\sSet}(\Delta_{c,1}, Y) \,.
        \end{equation*}
        Given $\alpha \colon [m] \to [n]$, the universal property of the pullback construction induces maps $\Delta_{c \alpha, i} \to \Delta_{c,i}$, and precomposition with these maps turns $X \star Y$ into a simplicial set.

    \subsection{Remark}\label{chVI-3-5}
        The join of two simplicial sets is slightly awkward to write down since we chose to define $\Delta$ as the category with object set $\{[n] \mid n \geq 0 \}$. Note that $\Delta$ is a skeleton of the category of all non-empty, finite linear orders $\LinOrd_{\fin}$, so
        \begin{equation*}
            \sSet \simeq \Fun(\LinOrd^{\op}_{\fin}, \Set) \,.
        \end{equation*}
        In the latter category, we could define the join of $X$ and $Y$ by
        \begin{equation*}
            (X \star Y)_n \coloneq \coprod\limits_{c \in \cut[n]} X(c^{-1}(0)) \times Y(c^{-1}(1)) \,.
        \end{equation*}
        This is not the only construction that is easier to define. With the enlarged indexing category, the functor $(-)^{\op}$ is for example simply induced by the endofunctor of $\LinOrd_{\fin}$ which reverses the ordering on each object.

    \subsection{Example}\label{chVI-3-6}
        By inspection, one observes that
        \begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*)]
            \item 
                $\Delta^0 \star \Delta^0 \cong \Delta^1$;
            \item 
                $X \star \varnothing \cong X$ and $\varnothing \star Y \cong Y$.
        \end{enumerate}

    \subsection{Lemma}\label{chVI-3-7}
        The join construction defines a functor $- \star - \colon \sSet \times \sSet \to \sSet$.
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}. Given maps $f \colon X \to X'$ and $g \colon Y \to Y'$, composition with $f$ and $g$ induces maps
        \begin{equation*}
            (X \star Y)_n \to (X' \star Y')_n
        \end{equation*}
        for all $n$, and it is straightforward to check that these define maps of simplicial sets.\hfill$\Box$

    \subsection{Corollary}\label{chVI-3-8}
        Let $X$ and $Y$ be simplicial sets. Then $X \star -$ and $- \star Y$ factor as
        \begin{equation*}
            X \star - \,\colon \sSet \to X / \sSet \to \sSet \quad \text{and} \quad - \star Y \,\colon \sSet \to Y / \sSet \to \sSet \,.
        \end{equation*}

        \emph{Proof}. By \ref{chVI-3-7}, the unique map $\varnothing \to Y$ induces a map $X \cong X \star \varnothing \to X \star Y$, which defines an object in $X / \sSet$. Functoriality is obvious. The other case is analogous.\hfill$\Box$

    \subsection{Lemma}\label{chVI-3-9}
        For every map of simplicial sets $p \colon X \to \Delta^1$, there exists a factorization
        \begin{equation*}
            X \xrightarrow{q} X_0 \star X_1 \xrightarrow{c} \Delta^1 \,,
        \end{equation*}
        where $X_i = p^{-1}(\{i\})$ and $c \colon X_0 \star X_1 \to \Delta^0 \star \Delta^0 \cong \Delta^1$ is induced by the projection maps. This factorization is initial among maps
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                X \arrow[dr] \arrow[rr] & & Y \star Z \arrow[dl] \\
                & \Delta^1 & 
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        such that $Y \star Z \to \Delta^1$ us induced by the projection maps to $\Delta^0$.
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}. We need to define $q$. Let $x \colon \Delta^n \to X$ be an $n$-simplex. Then the composition $p x$ determines a cut in $[n]$. Define
        \begin{equation*}
            q(x) \coloneq (\Delta_{px,0} \to \Delta^n \xrightarrow{x} X,\, \Delta_{px,1} \to \Delta^n \xrightarrow{x} X) \,.
        \end{equation*}
        It is straightforward to check that this defines a map of simplicial sets and that it is initial in the claimed sense.\hfill$\Box$

    \subsection{Example}\label{chVI-3-10}
        Let $\Cc$ and $\Dd$ be ordinary categories. The canonical projection $\Cc \star \Dd \to [1]$ induces by \ref{chVI-3-9} a factorization
        \begin{equation*}
            N(\Cc \star \Dd) \to N \Cc \star N \Dd \to \Delta^1 \,.
        \end{equation*}
        Since there exists an unique morphism from each object in $\Cc$ to each object in $\Dd$, it is easy to see that the first map is an isomorphism. So there is a natural isomorphism
        \begin{equation*}
            N(\Cc \star \Dd) \cong N \Cc \star N \Dd \,.
        \end{equation*}
        In particular, there are canonical isomorphisms
        \begin{equation*}
            \Delta^{n+m+1} \cong \Delta^n \star \Delta^m \,.
        \end{equation*}

    \subsection{Lemma}\label{chVI-3-11}
        If $\Cc$ and $\Dd$ are quasicategories, then $\Cc \star \Dd$ is also a quasicategory.
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}. Let $f \colon \horn{n}{k} \to \Cc \star \Dd$ be an inner horn. If $f$ factors via one of the maps $\Cc \to \Cc \star \Dd$ or $\Dd \to \Cc \star \Dd$, the map $f$ obviously extends. Note that this is the case if and only if the composite map $\horn{n}{k} \xrightarrow{f} \Cc \star \Dd \xrightarrow{p} \Delta^1$ is constant.\\
        So let us assume that $p f$ is not constant. Since $\Delta^1$ is the nerve of a category, $p f$ extends uniquely to a map $q \colon \Delta^n \to \Delta^1$ which we identify with a map $[n] \to [1]$. By \ref{chVI-3-9}, there is a factorization
        \begin{equation*}
            f \,\colon \horn{n}{k} \to L_0 \star L_1 \xrightarrow{c} \Cc \star \Dd \,.
        \end{equation*}
        A map $\alpha \colon [r] \to [n]$ defines a simplex in $L_0$ if and only if there exists some $i \ne k$ such that $i \notin \im(\alpha)$ and if $q \alpha$ takes the constant value $0$. Since $q$ is not constant, the latter condition implies that $n \notin \im(\alpha)$. As $n \ne k$, this automatically entails $\alpha \in \horn{n}{k}$. Consequently, $L_0 \cong \Delta^{\gr{q^{-1}(0)}}$.\\
        Similarly, if $q \alpha$ takes the constant value $1$, then $0 \notin \im(\alpha)$ and $\alpha \in \horn{n}{k}$ since $0 \ne k$. Therefore, $L_1 \cong \Delta^{\gr{c^{-1}(1)}-1}$.\\
        by \ref{chVI-3-10}, $L_0 \star L_1 \cong \Delta^n$, so $c$ is the desired extension of $f$.\hfill$\Box$

    \subsection{Lemma}\label{chVI-3-12}
        Let $\Cc$ be a category and $x \in \Cc$.
        \begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*)]
            \item 
                If $\Cc$ is cocomplete, then $x/\Cc$ and $\Cc/x$ are cocomplete. The forgetful functor $x/\Cc \to \Cc$ preserves connected colimits, the forgetful functor $\Cc/x \to \Cc$ preserves all colimits.
            \item 
                If $\Cc$ is complete, then $x/\Cc$ and $\Cc/x$ are complete. The forgetful functor $x/\Cc \to \Cc$ preserves all limits, the forgetful functor $\Cc/x \to \Cc$ preserves all connected limits.
        \end{enumerate}

        \emph{Proof}. We prove only that part of the lemma we will actually need. Suppose $\Cc$ is cocomplete and let $F \colon I \to x/\Cc$ be a diagram. Then $F$ defines a diagram $G \colon \{-\infty\} \star I \to \Cc$, which admits a colimit by assumption. The structure map $x = G(-\infty) \to \colim G$ defines an object in $x/\Cc$.\\
        Let $F^{+} \colon I \star \{\infty\} \to x/\Cc$ be an arbitrary cone under $F$. Then $F^{+}$ corresponds to a diagram $G^{+} \colon \{-\infty\} \star I \star \{\infty\} \to \Cc$, which is a cone under $G$. So there is an unique induced map $\colim G \to G^{+}(\infty)$, which is compatible with the structure maps $x \to \colim G$ and $x \to G^{+}(\infty)$ by definition. Therefore, $x \to \colim G$ is a colimit of $F$.\\
        Note that there is always a canonical map $\colim {G|}_I \to \colim G$. Let us try to turn $\colim {G|}_I$ into a cone under $G$. For $i \in I$, choose as $G(i) \to \colim {G|}_I$ the respective structure map of the colimit. Let $\sim$ be the equivalence relation on $I$ given by $i \sim j$ if the maps $x \to G(i) \to \colim {G|}_I$ and $x \to G(j) \to \colim {G|}_I$ are the same. By definition of the colimit, $i \sim j$ whenever there exists a morphism $i \to j$, so there exists only a single equivalence class if $I$ is connected. Consequently, we may pick any $i \in I$ and consider the composition $x \to G(i) \to \colim {G|}_I$ to exhibit $\colim {G|}_I$ as a cone under $G$. It follows that there exists an induced map $\colim G \to \colim {G|}_I$.\\
        Tracing through the definitions shows that both $\colim {G|}_I \to \colim G \to \colim {G|}_I$ and $\colim G \to \colim {G|}_I \to \colim G$ are the identity.\\
        For the other claims, see Land's ``Introduction to infinity-categories'', Lemma 4.13.\hfill$\Box$

    \subsection{Corollary}\label{chVI-3-13}
        Let $X \in \sSet$. Then $X \star - \colon \sSet \to X/\sSet$ and $- \star X \colon \sSet \to X/\sSet$ preserve all colimits.
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}. For $F \colon I \to \Cc$ an arbitrary diagram in a cocomplete category $\Cc$, note that
        \begin{equation*}
            \colim F \cong \coprod\limits_{J \in \pi_0(I)} \colim\limits_{i \in J} {F|}_{J} \,.
        \end{equation*}
        Hence, it suffices to show that $X \star -$ preserves coproducts and connected colimits.\\
        In $\Set$, observe that
        \begin{equation*}
            \Hom_{\Set}(\varnothing, \colim\limits_{I} Z) \cong \colim\limits_{I} \Hom_{\Set}(\varnothing, Z)
        \end{equation*}
        if and only if $I$ is connected.\\
        By \ref{chVI-3-12}, connected colimits may be computed in $\sSet$, and this can be done degreewise. Let $Y \colon I \to \sSet$ be a connected diagram. Using that simplices are tiny, the remark about colimits in $\Set$ (since $\Delta_{c,1} = \varnothing$ for $c$ the constant map $[n] \to \{0\} \to [1]$). and that all colimits in $\Set$ commute with products with a fixed set, we have
        \begin{align*}
            (X \star \colim\limits_{I} Y)_n &= \coprod\limits_{c \in \cut[n]} \Hom(\Delta_{c,0}, X) \times \Hom(\Delta_{c,1}, \colim\limits_{I} Y) \\
            &\cong \coprod\limits_{c \in \cut[n]} \colim\limits_{i \in I} \Hom(\Delta_{c,0}, X) \times \Hom(\Delta_{c,1}, Y(i)) \\
            &\cong \colim\limits_{I} \coprod\limits_{c \in \cut[n]} \Hom(\Delta_{c,0}, X) \times \Hom(\Delta_{c,1}, Y(i)) \\
            &\cong \colim\limits_{I}(X \star Y(i))_n \,.
        \end{align*}
        So $X \star -$ preserves all connected colimits.\\
        For coproducts, we saw in the proof of \ref{chVI-3-12} that $\coprod\limits_{i \in I}(X \to X \star Y(i))$ may be computed as the colimit of the associated diagram $\{-\infty\} \star I \to \sSet$. It is straightforward to verify that this colimit is precisely $X \star (\coprod\limits_{i \in I} Y(i))$.\hfill$\Box$
        \medskip

        Since $X \star - \colon \sSet \to X/\sSet$ and $- \star X \colon \sSet \to X/\sSet$ are colimit-preserving, it follows that they admit right adjoints.

    \subsection{Definition}\label{chVI-3-14}
        Let $f \colon X \to Y$. Define the \emph{slices} $Y_{f/}$ and $Y_{/f}$ of $Y$ under and over $f$ as the images of $f$ under the right adjoints $X/\sSet \to \sSet$ of $X \star -$ and $- \star X$, respectively.

    \subsection{Remark}\label{chVI-3-15}
        Note that slices encode cones under and over a given diagram $f$. So to $f \colon I \to \Cc$ a functor of categories, one musr be careful to distinguish the categories $N(f/\Cc)$ and $N(\Cc)_{N(f)/}$; they only agree when $I = *$.
        \medskip

        The reformulation of \hyperref[chIII-thm-c]{Theorem C} that we are planning to prove next is the following (note that we are cheating a little bit in the formulation since we have not yet discussed the functoriality of the slice construction):

    \subsection{Theorem}\label{chVI-3-16}
        Let $\Cc$ be a quasicategory and let $f \colon x \to y$ be a morpism in $\Cc$. Then $f$ is an equivalence if and only if
        \begin{equation*}
            \Cc_{f/} \to \Cc_{x/}
        \end{equation*}
        is a trivial Kan fibration.
        \medskip

        This will be done in January. On the way to proving this theorem, we will consider a pushout-joint-construction $\boxstar$ which is analogous to the pushout-product-construction $\boxtimes$.

    \subsection{Notation}\label{chVI-3-17}
        For $i \colon A \to B$ and $f \colon X \to Y$, denote by $i \boxstar f$ the map
        \begin{equation*}
            (A \star Y) \underset{A \star X}{\cup} (B \star X) \to B \star Y
        \end{equation*}
        induced by $i$ and $f$.
        \medskip

        The key technical statement about this construction is the following:

    \subsection{Lemma}\label{chVI-3-18}
        Let $i \colon A \to B$ and $j \colon K \to L$ be injections of simplicial sets.
        \begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*)]
            \item 
                $i \boxstar j$ is an injection.
            \item 
                If $i$ is right anodyne, then $i \boxstar j$ is inner anodyne.
            \item 
                If $j$ is left anodyne, then $i \boxstar j$ is inner anodyne.
            \item 
                If $i$ is left anodyne, then $i \boxstar j$ is left anodyne.
            \item 
                If $j$ is right anodyne, then $i \boxstar j$ is right anodyne.
        \end{enumerate}

        Just like \ref{chVI-3-16}, the proof has to wait until next year. But we can use it to close the remaining gap in Chapter V:

        Recall that $\Box^n$ denotes the $n$-cube $(\Delta^1)^n$, and that
        \begin{equation*}
            \partial \Box^n \coloneq \{ \alpha \colon [k] \to [1]^n \mid \exists 1 \leq i \leq n \colon \pr_i \alpha \text{ constant}\}
        \end{equation*}
        is the boundary of the $n$-cube. In order to identify our cubical model $\pi_{*}(X, x)$ of simplicial homotopy groups with the simplicial model $\pi^{\triangle}_{*}(X, x)$, we used the following statement:

    \subsection{Lemma}\label{chVI-3-19}
        For $n \geq 2$, let $C^n \subseteq \Box^n$ denote the simplicial subset obtained by removing the simplex
        \begin{equation*}
            \sigma^n \,\colon [n] \to [1]^n, \quad i \mapsto \underbrace{1 \ldots 1}_{i \text{ times}} \; \underbrace{0 \ldots 0}_{n-i \text{ times}} \,.
        \end{equation*}
        Then the inclusion $\partial \Box^n \to C^n$ is inner anodyne.
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}. The case $n = 2$ holds by inspection.\\
        For $k \geq 1$, set
        \begin{equation*}
            L_k \coloneq \{ \alpha \colon [p] \to [1]^k \mid \underbrace{1 \ldots 1}_{k \text{ times}} \notin \im(\alpha)\} \subseteq \Box^k
        \end{equation*}
        and
        \begin{equation*}
            L'_k \coloneq \{ \alpha \colon [p] \to [1]^k \mid \underbrace{0 \ldots 0}_{k \text{ times}} \notin \im(\alpha)\} \subseteq \Box^k \,.
        \end{equation*}
        Note that $L_k$ already appeared in \ref{chV-4-14} and the proof of \ref{chV-4-15}. Set $S_k \coloneq L_k \cap L'_k$. Observe that there is a canonical isomorphism
        \begin{equation*}
            (\{1\} \times L_k) \underset{\{1\} \times S_k}{\cup} (\Delta^1 \times S_k) \underset{\{0\} \times S_k}{\cup} (\{0\} \times L'_k) \xrightarrow{\cong} S_{k+1} \\
            (\tau, \alpha) \mapsto (i \mapsto \tau(i) \frown \alpha(i)) \,.
        \end{equation*}
        Then
        \begin{equation*}
            (\{1\} \to \Delta^1) \boxtimes (S_k \to L_k)
        \end{equation*}
        is right anodyne. Consequently, the lower horizontal map in the pushout square
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                (\Delta^1 \times S_k) \underset{\{1\} \times S_k}{\cup} (\{1\} \times L_k) \arrow[d] \arrow[r] & L_k \times \Delta^1 \arrow[d] \\
                S_{k+1} \arrow[r] & X_{k+1}
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        is also right anodyne. Note that the induced map $X \to \Box^{k+1}$ is injective, so we can regard $X_{k+1}$ as a simplicial subset of $\Box^{k+1}$. \\
        Let now $n > 2$. The simplicial subset $\{0\} \times \partial \Box^{n-1}$ is contained in $X_n$, so we can form the pushout
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \{0\} \times \partial \Box^{n-1} \arrow[d] \arrow[r] & \{0\} \times C^{n-1} \arrow[d] \\
                X_n \arrow[r] & Y_n
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        By induction, the bottom horizontal map is inner anodyne. Note again that $Y_n$ is a simplicial subset of $\Box^n$. It follows that $S_n \to X_n \to Y_n$ is right anodyne.\\
        By \ref{chVI-3-18}, the map
        \begin{equation*}
            (S_n \to Y_n) \boxstar (\varnothing \to \{1 \ldots 1\})
        \end{equation*}
        is inner anodyne. Finally, the square
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                (S_n \star \{1 \ldots 1\}) \underset{S_n \star \varnothing}{\cup} \arrow[d] \arrow[r] & Y_n \star \{1 \ldots 1\} \arrow[d] \\
                \partial \Box^n \arrow[r] & C^n
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        is a pushout, so we see that $\partial \Box^n \to C^n$ is inner anodyne as claimed.\hfill$\Box$
        \medskip

        This closes the remaining gap in the proof of \ref{chV-3-14}.
        \medskip

        The goal of the present section id to prove:

    \subsection{Theorem (Joyal's lifting theorem)}\label{chVI-3-20}
        Let $F \colon \Cc \to \Dd$ be an inner fibration between quasicategories. Then all outer horns
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \horn{n}{0} \arrow[d] \arrow[r, "f"] & \Cc \arrow[d, "F"] \\
                \Delta^n \arrow[ur, dashed] \arrow[r] & \Dd
            \end{tikzcd}
            \qquad\qquad\quad
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \horn{n}{n} \arrow[d] \arrow[r, "g"] & \Cc \arrow[d] \\
                \Delta^n \arrow[ur, dashed] \arrow[r] & \Dd
            \end{tikzcd}  
        \end{equation*}
        with
        \begin{equation*}
            \Delta^1 \xrightarrow{\text{incl}} \horn{n}{0} \xrightarrow{f} \Cc \quad \text{or} \quad \Delta^1 \xrightarrow{(n-1,n)} \horn{n}{n} \xrightarrow{g} \Cc
        \end{equation*}
        representing equivalences in $\Cc$ admit a contractible space of lifts.
        \medskip

        The proof will be by reduction to the ? (also to be proven \ref{chVI-3-16} and \ref{chVI-3-17}). But before that, let's get back into the groove with some corollaries to see the importance of this result.

    \subsection{Corollary (Theorem C)}\label{chVI-3-21}
        Every anima is a Kan complex. In particular, the core of any quasicategory is a Kan complex.\\
        We already recorded the converse in \ref{chIII-8}.\\
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}. Apply \ref{chVI-3-20} with $\Dd = \pt$ to obtain fillers for the outer horns.\hfill$\Box$

    \subsection{Corollary (Second half of Theorem D)}\label{chVI-3-22}
        For quasicategories $\Cc$ and $\Dd$,
        \begin{equation*}
            \Fun(\Cc, \core \Dd) \subseteq \core(\Fun(\Cc, \Dd))
        \end{equation*}
        is a collection of path components, with equality if either $\Cc$ or $\Dd$ is an anima.
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}. Since $\core \Dd$ is an anima, it is a Kan complex by the previous corollary, so $\Fun(\Cc, \core \Dd)$ is a Kan complex by \ref{chV-2-24}, thus an anima by (the trivial) \ref{chIII-8}. This also gives the containment and furthermore equality if $\Dd$ is an anima.\\
        For the remainder, observe first that a functor naturally equivalent to one taking values in the core of $\Dd$ also does so, as this can (by definition of the core and \ref{chIII-8})be tested in the homotopy category, where it is obvious. To see that it is an inclusion of path components, take an $n$-simplex $\sigma$ on the right, whose vertices lie in $\Fun(\Cc, \core \Dd)$. \\
        We have to show that already $\sigma \in \Fun(\Cc, \core \Dd)$. This means that regarded as a map $\Delta^n \times \Cc \to \Dd$ all edges of $\Delta^n \times \Cc$ map to equivalences in $\Dd$. Again this can be tested in the homotopy category, where for an arbitrary edge $(f, g)$ we find $[(f, g)] = [(f, \id)] \circ [(\id, g)]$. But by assumption the edges of $\sigma$ are natural equivalences which in particular means that edges of the form $(f, \id)$ are mapped to equivalences. And also by assumption the vertices of $\sigma$ are functors into the core, so edges of the form $(\id, g)$ are mapped to equivalences. Thus we generally have an inclusion of path components with equality if $\Cc$ is anima, as then any functor $\Cc \to \Dd$ factors through $\core \Dd$.\hfill$\Box$
        \medskip

        Of course the inclusion
        \begin{equation*}
            \Fun(\Cc, \core \Dd) \subseteq \core(\Fun(\Cc, \Dd))
        \end{equation*}
        is generally strict, and we do not yet have a handle on the target. However, already the fact that transformations between functors to an anima are invertible os far from obvious. Decoding we obtain that a natural transformation $\tau \colon F \Rightarrow G$ of functors $\Cc \to \Dd$ is invertible if for every $f \colon x \to y$ in $\Cc$ the diagram
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                F(x) \arrow[d, "F(f)"'] \arrow[r, "\tau_x"] & G(x) \arrow[d, "G(f)"] \\
                F(y) \arrow[r, "\tau_y"] & G(y)
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        consists entirely of equivalences, then $\tau$ is invertible.\\
        Clearly, the invertibility of $G(f)$ and $F(f)$ should not play a role. That is the content of \hyperref[chIII-thm-e]{Theorem E} and will have to wait until the next section.\\
        However, we now know that $\Core F(-, -)$ gives a Kan-enrichment on the category $\hCat{q} \subseteq \sSet$.

    \subsection{Definition}\label{chVI-3-23}
        Define the \emph{quasicategory of quasicategories}, usually denoted by $\Cat_{\infty}$, as $\Nn^c(\hCat{q})$ using the above enrichment.
        \medskip

        Since $F(\Cc, K)$ is Kan if $K$ is, we also obtain a simplicially enriched functor
        \begin{equation*}
            \Kan \to \hCat{q}
        \end{equation*}
        and so a functor $\An \to \Cat_{\infty}$. It is degree-wise injective and gives bijections $\Hom_{\An}(K, K') \to \Hom_{\Cat_{\infty}}(K, K')$ for every two anima $K$ and $K'$.\\
        Let ne warn you about two things though: We do not yet know the homotopy type of either of these spaces (that is the content of \hyperref[chIV-thm-h]{Theorem H}), but even assuming it we find
        \begin{equation*}
            \Hom_{\Cat_{\infty}}(\Cc, \Cc') \simeq \Core \Fun(\Cc, \Cc') \,.
        \end{equation*}
        So $\Cat_{\infty}$ is neither analogous to $\Cat$, the $1$-category of small $1$-categories, nor $\Cat_2$, the $2$-category of small $1$-categories, but rather the $(2, 1)$-category obtained from $\Cat_2$ by discarding non-invertible natural transformations. Indeed, $\hCat{q}$ really is $\hCat{q}$ enriched and thus wants to give a (non-strict) $(\infty, 2)$-category. Alas, we shall not define such objects (they are not nearly as developed as quasicategories), apart from mentioning that they can be defined using either enriched $(\infty, 1)$-category theory, or by iterating Rezk's Segal space approach to higher categories.\\
        Let us, however, record that equivalences in $\Cat_{\infty}$ are exactly the equivalences of quasicategories.
        \medskip

        Here's another application of anima being Kan complexes: Let $\Jj \in \Kan$ denote the nerve of the groupoid having objects $0, 1$ and 4 morphisms as indicated on the picture:
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                0 \arrow[loop, distance=2em, in=215, out=145, "\id"'] \arrow[r, bend left] & 1 \arrow[loop, distance=2em, in=35, out=325, "\id"'] \arrow[l, bend left]
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        There is then a canonical inclusion $\Delta^1 \subseteq \Jj$ and we have:

    \subsection{Proposition}\label{chVI-3-24}
        A morphism $f$ in a quasicategory $\Cc$ is an equivalence if and only if its classifier $\Delta^1 \to \Cc$ lies in the image of the restriction
        \begin{equation*}
            p \,\colon \Fun(\Jj, \Cc) \to \Fun(\Delta^1, \Cc) \,.
        \end{equation*}
        In this case the core of the fibre over $f$ is contractible.
        \medskip

        This proposition says that inverses of an equivalence in a quasicategory are unique up to contractible choice!\\
        The statement follows from \ref{chVI-3-21} and the following Lemma:

    \subsection{Lemma}\label{chVI-3-25}
        The map $\Delta^1 \to \Jj$ is both left and right anodyne.
        \medskip

        The complex $\Jj$ has exactly 2 non-degenerate simplices in each dimension (corresponding to the sequences $0 \to 1 \to 0 \to 1 \ldots$ and $1 \to 0 \to 1 \to 0 \ldots$). Its realization is easily checked to be $S^{\infty}$ with the ``hemispherical'' cell structure, in particular, contractible, whence \ref{chV-4-4} shows that $\Delta^1 \subseteq \Jj$ is at least anodyne, which would suffice for our purposes.\\
        But it is clearly not inner anodyne (it is not even an equivalence of categories), so gives a weak example that
        \begin{equation*}
            \text{inner anodynes} \subsetneq \text{left anodynes} \,\cap\, \text{right anodynes} \,.
        \end{equation*}

        \emph{Proof}. To see that the inclusion is left anodyne, let $F_k \subseteq \Jj$ be the simplicial subset generated by the $k$-simplex $\upsilon_k = 0 \to 1 \to 0 \to 1 \to 0 \to \ldots$. Then $\Delta^1 = F_1$ and there is a pushout diagram
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}[row sep=small]
                \horn{k}{0} \arrow[d] \arrow[r] & F_{k-1} \arrow[d, phantom, "\subseteq" rotate=-90] \\
                \Delta^k \arrow[r, "\upsilon_k"] & F_k
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        since $v_k \colon \Delta^k \to F_k$ is surjective and $\upsilon_k^{-1}(F_{k-1}) = \horn{k}{0}$, because $d_i \upsilon_k$ is degenerate for $0 < i < k$ (and $\sk_{k-2} F_{k-1} = \sk_{k-2} \Jj$, which contains all degenerate $k-1$ simplices), $d_k \upsilon_k = \upsilon_{k-1} \in F_{k-1}$ and $d_0 \upsilon_k = 1 \to 0 \to 1 \to \ldots \notin F_{k-1}$. The claim follows.\\
        To see that $\Delta^1 \subseteq \Jj$ is also right anodyne use the filtration spanned by the simplices $\ldots 0 \to 1 \to 0 \to 1$ instead.\hfill$\Box$
        \medskip

        \emph{Proof of \ref{chVI-3-24}}. Since $\Jj$ is an anima any map $\Jj \to \Cc$ factors through $\core \Cc$, so both its edges go to equivalences.\\
        Conversely, if $f$ is an equivalence it is represented by a map $\Delta^1 \to \core \Cc$, which is a Kan complex by \ref{chVI-3-21}. But then the map
        \begin{equation*}
            \overline{p} \,\colon \Fun(\Jj, \core \Cc) \to \Fun(\Delta^1, \core \Cc)
        \end{equation*}
        is a trivial Kan fibration by \ref{chVI-3-25} and \ref{chV-2-26}, in particular, surjective on $0$-simplices. To see the contractibility statement, note that
        \begin{equation*}
            \Fun(\Jj, \core \Cc) = \core \Fun(\Jj, \Cc)
        \end{equation*}
        by \ref{chVI-3-22} and that
        \begin{equation*}
            \core p^{-1}(f) = p^{-1}(f) \cap \core \Fun(\Jj, \Cc) = \overline{p}^{-1}(f) \,;
        \end{equation*}
        to see the first equality let $h \colon \Delta^1 \to \Fun()\Jj, \Cc$ be inverse to $g \colon \Delta^1 \to p^{-1}(f)$. Then $p(h)$ is inverse to $p(g) = \id_f$ so $p(h) \sim \id_f$. But $p$ is an inner fibration by \ref{chVI-2-4} so then we can fill
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \horn{2}{1} \arrow[d] \arrow[r, "{(h, \id)}"] & \Fun(\Jj, \Cc) \arrow[d, "p"] \\
                \Delta^2 \arrow[ur, dashed, "G"] \arrow[r, "H"] & \Fun(\Delta^1, \Cc)
            \end{tikzcd}
            \qquad
            H =
            \begin{tikzcd}[column sep=small]
                & 1 \arrow[dr, "\id_f"] & \\
                0 \arrow[ur, "{p(h)}"] \arrow[rr, "\id_f"] & & 2
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        and $d_1 G \sim h$ is still inverse to $g$, but also $d_1 G \colon \Delta^1 \to p^{-1}(f)$, so $g \in \core p^{-1}(f)$.\\
        Finally, $\overline{p}^{-1}(f)$ is contractible by \ref{chV-3-17}.\hfill$\Box$

    \subsection{Remark}\label{chVI-3-26}
        In fact, something stronger is true: The map $p \colon \Fun(\Jj, \Cc) \to \Fun(\Delta^1, \Cc)$ is a trivial Kan fibration, and thus an equivalence of categories, onto the subcategory of the target spanned by the equivalences. This will be an exercise later. In particular, $p^{-1}(f)$ is itself a contractible Kan complex! The latter statement also follows from \ref{chVI-3-24} and the pointwise criterion for natural equivalences, which implies that $p$ is conservative.
        \medskip

        Continuing with applications, we have:

    \subsection{Corollary}\label{chVI-3-27}
        If $p \colon \Cc \to \Dd$ is left or right fibration and $\Dd$ is Kan, then $p$ is a Kan fibration as well. In particular, $\Cc$ is also a Kan complex. \\
        Specializing to $\Dd = *$ we see that left and right Kan complexes are automatically Kan complexes, as announced in \ref{chV-1-6}. In particular, the fibres of left/right fibrations are always Kan complexes, removing an assumption from Exercise 4, sheet 8, that constructed a functor
        \begin{equation*}
            \st_p \,\colon h\Dd \to \hoAn
        \end{equation*}
        from a left fibration $p \colon \Cc \to \Dd$. From this point of view \ref{chVI-3-22} is also very natural. If $\Dd$ is Kan then $h\Dd$ is a groupoid, so $\st_p$ factors through core $\hoAn$, as does the straightening of any Kan fibration. In fact, it is an exercise on sheet 10 to show that this characterizes Kan fibrations among left fibrations.

    \subsection{Lemma / Definition}\label{chVI-3-28}
        Left and right fibrations are \emph{conservative}, i.e. if $p \colon \Cc \to \Dd$ is a left/right fibration between quasicategories and $f \in \Cc_1$ is a morphism such that $p(f)$ is an equivalence, then so is $f$ itself. In particular, if $\Dd$ is an  anima then so is $\Cc$.
        \smallskip

        The proof is another exercise on sheet 10; crucially it does not use Joyal's lifting theorem, as it will also enter into its proof!
        \medskip

        \emph{Proof of \ref{chVI-3-27}}. Given a lifting diagram
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \horn{n}{i} \arrow[d] \arrow[r] & \Cc \arrow[d, "p"] \\
                \Delta^n \arrow[r] & \Dd
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        with $i \in \{0, n\}$, then we know from \ref{chVI-3-26} that $\Cc$ is an anima as $\Dd$ is one. Thus \ref{chVI-3-20} applies to give a lift.\hfill$\Box$
        \medskip

        Ley me spell out explicitly another corollary on the technical side:

    \subsection{Corollary}\label{chVI-3-29}
        If $p \colon \Cc \to \Dd$ is a left (or right) fibration with contractible fibres, then it is a trivial Kan fibration.
        \medskip

        This generalizes \ref{chV-3-18}, where we showed the analogous assertion for Kan fibrations. Let me warn you, though: A left fibration, that is a weak homotopy equivalence need not be a Joyal equivalence, let alone a trivial fibration. Counterexamples follow below.
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}. We follow the proof of \ref{chV-3-18}: Given a diagram
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \partial \Delta^n \arrow[d] \arrow[r] & \Cc \arrow[d, "p"] \\
                \Delta^n \arrow[r] & \Dd
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        consider a simplicial contraction $H \colon \Delta^n \times \Delta^1 \to \Delta^n$ onto $\{n\}$. Then in the diagram
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}[row sep=small]
                \partial \Delta^n \times \{0\} \arrow[d, phantom, "\subseteq" rotate=-90] \arrow[rr] & & \Cc \arrow[d] \\
                \partial \Delta^n \times \Delta^1 \arrow[urr, dashed] \arrow[r, "H"] & \Delta^n \arrow[r] & \Dd
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        the left vertical map is left anodyne by \ref{chV-2-9}, so there exists a filler $G$. Now ${G|}_{\partial \Delta^n \times \{1\}}$ factors through a single fibre, so by assumption $G$ can be extended to a diagram
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \partial \Delta^n \times \Delta^1 \cup \Delta^n \times \{1\} \arrow[d] \arrow[rr, "G"] & & \Cc \arrow[d] \\
                \Delta^n \times \Delta^1 \arrow[urr, dashed] \arrow[r, "H"] & \Delta^n \arrow[r] & \Dd
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        which in \ref{chV-2-9} we could fill since the left hand map is right anodyne (by definition). The restriction of a filler to $\Delta^n \times \{1\}$ in any case is a solution to the original lifting problem. This time we have to use Joyal's theorem: First, as part of the proof of \ref{chV-2-9}, we have seen that the inclusion
        \begin{equation*}
            \partial \Delta^n \times \Delta^1 \cup \Delta^n \times \{1\} \subseteq \Delta^n \times \Delta^1 - \{\alpha_n \cup d_{n+1} \alpha_n\}
        \end{equation*}
        is actually inner anodyne, where $\alpha_i \colon [n+1] \to [n] \times [1]$ is given by
        \begin{equation*}
            k \mapsto
            \begin{cases}
                (k, 0) & k \leq i \\
                (k - 1, 1) & k > i
            \end{cases}
        \end{equation*}
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzpicture}[
                scale=0.9, 
                thick, 
                color=violet, 
                every node/.style={text=black}
            ]
                    \coordinate (A) at (0, 0);
                    \coordinate (B) at (0, 2);
                    \coordinate (C) at (2, 2);
                    \coordinate (D) at (2, 0);

                    \node[below] at (A) {$01$};
                    \node[above] at (B) {$00$};
                    \node[above] at (C) {$10$};                    
                    \node[below] at (D) {$11$};

                    \draw[dashed,postaction={nomorepostaction,decorate,decoration={markings,mark=at position 0.6 with {\arrow{>[length=8pt,width=5pt]}}}}] (B) -- (C);

It is a theorem of Joyal that the natural map $\Cc \big/ X \to \Cc_{/x}$ is always a Joyal equivalence. We may discuss this later, or next term.

    \subsection{Lemma}\label{chVI-3-35}
        There are canonical isomorphisms
        \begin{equation*}
            (\horn{n}{j} \to \Delta^n) \boxstar (\partial \Delta^m \to \Delta^m) \cong \horn{n+1+m}{j} \to \Delta^{n+1+m}
        \end{equation*}
        and
        \begin{equation*}
            (\partial \Delta^m \to \Delta^m) \boxstar (\horn{n}{j} \to \Delta^n) \cong \horn{m+1+n}{m+1+j} \to \Delta^{m+1+n}
        \end{equation*}
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}: Since joining preserves (by definition) injections, it also preserves unions of simplicial subsets, i.e. for $A, B \subseteq X$ we have
        \begin{align*}
            (A \cup B) \star Y = (A \star Y) \cup (B \star Y) \subseteq X \star Y \\
            Y \star (A \cup B) = (Y \star A) \cup (Y \star B) \subseteq Y \star X
        \end{align*}
        as the image of either term in $X \star Y$ can be determined using the pushout of $A \leftarrow \varnothing \rightarrow B$, which $- \star Y$ and $Y \star -$ preserve by \ref{chVI-3-12} and \ref{chVI-3-13}.\\
        Thus we have:
        \begin{align*}
            \horn{n}{i} \star \Delta^m &= (\bigcup_{\substack{0 \leq j \leq n \\ i \neq j}} d_j(\Delta^{n-1})) \star \Delta^m = \bigcup_{\substack{0 \leq j \leq n \\ i \neq j}} (d_j(\Delta^{n-1}) \star \Delta^m) \\
            &= \bigcup_{\substack{0 \leq j \leq n \\ i \neq j}} d_j(\Delta^{n+m}) \subseteq \Delta^{n+1+m}
        \end{align*}
        Similarly, we have
        \begin{align*}
            \Delta^n \star \partial \Delta^m &= \Delta^n \star \bigcup_{0 \leq j \leq m} d_j(\Delta^{m-1}) \\
            &= \bigcup_{0 \leq j \leq m} \Delta^n \star d_j(\Delta^{m-1}) \\
            &= \bigcup_{0 \leq j \leq m} d_{n+1+j}(\Delta^{n+1+m-1})
            &= \bigcup_{n+1 \leq j \leq n+1+m} d_j(\Delta^{n+m}) \subseteq \Delta^{n+1+m}
        \end{align*}
        Their union is clearly $\horn{n+1+m}{i}$ and since $\boxstar$ preserves injections (again straight from the definition) the first claim follows. The second is analogous.\hfill$\Box$

            \end{tikzcd}	
        \end{equation*}
        In our case $A_0 \rightarrow A_1 = \Delta^0 \xrightarrow{d_1} \Delta^1 = \horn{1}{0} \to \Delta^1$ is left anodyne, so it would be enough to see that the right hand map in the upper diagram is a left fibration. It is \emph{not}! We will see that it is instead a right fibration. This will turn out to be good enough! Since $B_0 \to B_1 = \partial \Delta^{n-2} \to \Delta^{n-2}$, setting $Y = \star$ in the right diagram (together with \ref{chVI-3-30}) recovers the formulation \ref{chVI-3-16}, that we gave earlier.
        \medskip

        Let us first deal with the last argument:

    \subsection{Lemma}\label{chVI-3-36}
        Let $F \colon \Cc \to \Dd$ be an inner fibration of quasicategories. Then if $f \colon x \to y$ and $g \colon y \to x$ are inverse equivalences there exists a solution to one of the lifting problems
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \Delta^0 \arrow[d, hook, "d_1"] \arrow[r] & \Cc \arrow[d, "F"] \\
                \Delta^1 \arrow[ru, dashed] \arrow[r, "f"'] & \Dd
            \end{tikzcd}
            \qquad
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \Delta^0 \arrow[d, hook, "d_0"] \arrow[r] & \Cc \arrow[d, "F"] \\
                \Delta^1 \arrow[ru, dashed] \arrow[r, "g"'] & \Dd
            \end{tikzcd}	
        \end{equation*}	
        that represents an equivalence in $\Cc$, if and only if the same is true for the other.

    \subsection{Definition}\label{chVI-3-37}
        An inner fibration admitting lifts as above for all equivalences $f$ and $g$ is called an \emph{isofibration}.
        \medskip

        \emph{Proof of \ref{chVI-3-36}}: Assume the left lifting problem has a solution $h \colon a \to b$. Since $h$ represents an equivalence in $\Cc$ we can shoose an inverse $k$ to it. Since $F(h) = f$ we find $F(k)$ inverse to $f$, so there exists an $H \colon \Delta^2 \to \Dd$ with $d_0 H =0 secs F(k)$, $d_2 H = \id_Y$ and $d_1 H = g$. But then the lifting problem
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \horn{2}{1} \arrow[d] \arrow[r, "(\id_Y{,} k)"] & \Cc \arrow[d] \\
                \Delta^2 \arrow[ru, dashed] \arrow[r, "H"] & \Dd
            \end{tikzcd}	
        \end{equation*}	
        admits a solution $G$ and $d_1 G$ is then a lift of $g$ and an equivalence since it is homotopic to the equivalence $k$.\hfill$\Box$

    \subsection{Observation}\label{chVI-3-38}
        Both left and right fibrations between quasicategories are isofibrations.
        \medskip

        This statement will give the existence part of Joyal's theorem. We will use this to prove a refinement of this lemma below, from which we will deduce uniqueness.
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}: For a left fibration the left of the two lifting problems admits a solution by definition even without assuming $f$ an equivalence. But if it is an equivalence so is any lift by \ref{chVI-3-28}.\hfill$\Box$
        \medskip

        Next we discuss the slice construction o little further, as its functoriality is a bit funny: Recall that we have functors $\ls_K, \rs_K$
        \begin{equation*}
            K \big/ \sSet \to \sSet \quad (X, f) \mapsto X_{f/} \text{ and } X_{/f}
        \end{equation*}
        right adjoint to
        \begin{equation*}
            K \star - \text{ and } - \star K \colon \sSet \to K \big/ \sSet \,.
        \end{equation*}
        Given a map $\varphi \colon K \to L$, we obtain the functors
        \begin{equation*}
            \varphi^\star \colon L \big/ \sSet \to K \big/ \sSet \xrightarrow{\ls_K, \rs_K} \sSet \,.
        \end{equation*}	
        These composites admit a natural transformation from $\ls_L, rs_L$: For a map $f \colon L \to X$ a map
        \begin{equation*}
            X_{/f} \to X_{/f \varphi}
        \end{equation*}
        for example, is the same as a map
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}[column sep=small]
                & K \arrow[ld, hook] \arrow[rd, "f \varphi"] & \\
                X_{/f} \star K \arrow[rr, dashed] & & X 
            \end{tikzcd}	
        \end{equation*}		
        by adjunction. But the adjunction
        \begin{equation*}
            X_{/f} \star L \to X
        \end{equation*}
        provides such a map after precomposition with $\varphi$.\\
        The same argument gives the transformation for the left slice functor and I leave it to the reader to check naturality. In summary, the associations
        \begin{equation*}
            (X \xrightarrow{f} Y) \mapsto Y_{/f} \text{ and } Y_{f/}
        \end{equation*}
        are covariant in the target of $f$ but contravariant in the source. To encode this we have

    \subsection{Definition}\label{chVI-3-31}
        If $\Cc$ is a category define $\TwAr(\Cc)$, the \emph{twisted arrow category} of $\Cc$, to have objects the arrows of $\Cc$ and morphisms $f \to g$ pairs of arrows $(p, q) \in \mor \Cc^2$ such that
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                X \arrow[r, "f"] & Y \arrow[d, "q"] \\
                X' \arrow[u, "p"] \arrow[r, "g"] & Y'
            \end{tikzcd}	
        \end{equation*}
        commutes. Composition is just component-wise composition of arrows.
        \medskip

        Note that in distinction with the source and target evaluation in the arrow category
        \begin{equation*}
            (s, t) \colon \Ar (\Cc)\footnotemark \to \Cc \times \Cc
        \end{equation*}
        \footnotetext{$\Ar(\Cc) = \Fun(\mathbbmsl{N}[1], \Cc)$}
        these combine in to a functor
        \begin{equation*}
            (s, t) \colon \TwAr (\Cc) \to \Cc^{\op} \times \Cc \,.
        \end{equation*}	
        It is also readily checked that $\TwAr(\Cc)$ is nothing but the category of elements $Y \big/ \Hom_{\Cc}$ of the functor
        \begin{equation*}
            \Hom_{\Cc} \colon \Cc^{\op} \times \Cc \to \Set
        \end{equation*}
        We find:

    \subsection{Observation}\label{chVI-3-40}
        The slice constructions assemble into functors
        \begin{equation*}
            \ls, \rs \colon \TwAr(\sSet) \to \sSet \,.
        \end{equation*}
        In particular, for every sequence of map
        \begin{equation*}
            A \xrightarrow{i} X \xrightarrow{f} Y \xrightarrow{p} B
        \end{equation*}	
        we obtain maps
        \begin{align*}
            Y_{f /} \to Y_{f i /} \underset{B_{p f i /}}{\times} B_{p f /} \\
            Y_{/ f} \to Y_{/ f i} \underset{B_{/ p f i}}{\times} B_{p f} \,.
        \end{align*}
        It is these maps that appear in the proof of Joyal's theorem.
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}: The first part is immediate from the discussion preceding \ref{chVI-3-39}. For the second one we need to show that
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                Y_{f/} \arrow[d, "p_{\star}"] \arrow[r, "i^{\star}"] & Y_{f i /} \arrow[d, "p_{\star}"] \\
                B_{p f /} \arrow[r, "i^{\star}"] & B_{p f i /}
            \end{tikzcd}	
        \end{equation*}
        commutes. But this is immediate from the compositions
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                X \arrow[r, "f"] & Y \arrow[d, equal] \\
                A \arrow[d, equal] \arrow[u, "i"] \arrow[r, "f i"] & Y \arrow[d, "p"] \\
                A \arrow[r, "p f i"] & B
            \end{tikzcd}
            \quad \text{and} \quad
            \begin{tikzcd}
                X \arrow[d, equal] \arrow[r, "f"] & Y \arrow[d, "p"] \\
                X \arrow[r, "p f"] & B \arrow[d, equal] \\
                A \arrow[u] \arrow[r, "p f i"] & B
            \end{tikzcd}	
        \end{equation*}
        representing the same morphism $f \to p f i$ in $\TwAr(\sSet)$.\hfill$\Box$
        \medskip

        Let us now address the fibrancy properties of slices. Just as those of function spaces rested in the cofibrancy properties of the pushout-product operation, so do they rest on the cofibrancy properties of the pushout-join, which we recorded in \ref{chVI-3-18}, but did not yet prove. Here's the statement again:
        \medskip

        \ref{chVI-3-18} Lemma:
        Let $i \colon A \to B$ and $j \colon K \to L$ be cofibrations. Then $i \boxstar j$ is again a cofibration and
        \begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*)]
            \item[ii)-iii)]\addtocounter{enumi}{3}
                inner anodyne if either $i$ is right anodyne or $j$ is left anodyne
            \item
                left anodyne if $i$ is left anodyne
            \item
                right anodyne if $j$ is right anodyne
        \end{enumerate}
        \medskip

        We need one more lemma:

    \subsection{Lemma}\label{chVI-3-41}
        If $\Sigma \subseteq \mor (\sSet)$ is a saturated class of morphisms then
        \begin{equation*}
            \{ i \in \mor (\sSet) \mid i \boxstar j \in \Sigma \}
        \end{equation*}
        and
        \begin{equation*}
            \{ i \in \mor (\sSet)\ \mid j \boxstar i \in \Sigma \}
        \end{equation*}
        whenever $\Sigma$ is of the form
        \begin{equation*}
            \Sigma = \{ i \in \mor (\sSet) \mid i \text{ admits left lifts against } J \}
        \end{equation*}
        for some class $J \subseteq \mor (\sSet)$.
        \smallskip

        Note that \ref{chV-2-31} says that $\Sigma = \sat (S)$ for some set $S \subseteq \mor (\sSet)$ with compact sources satisfies the assumptions; in particular this is the case for $\Sigma$ the left/right/inner/$\varnothing$ anodyne maps.

    \subsection{Remark}\label{chVI-3-42}
        The analogue of this result for the pushout-product was exercise 3 on sheet 7. It was easier in that for every saturated class $\Sigma \in \mor (\sSet)$ the set
        \begin{equation*}
            \{ i \in \mor (\sSet) \mid i \boxstar j \in \Sigma \}
        \end{equation*}
        is again saturated, without the assumption that $\Sigma$ was defined by left lifting against some class $J$. The proof of that statement transfers to the present situation to show closure under retracts (trivially), pushouts and countable compositions, but not coproducts, since the join functor, when regarded as a functor $\sSet \to \sSet$ only preserves connected colimits.\\
        There are other sufficient conditions on $\Sigma$ such as right cancelability against cofibrations, i.e. if $i \in \Sigma$, $j$ cofibration and $i \circ j \in \Sigma$ implies $j \in \Sigma$. This is also true in all relevant examples , but nontrivial for all three cases of interest (though we know it for anodynes: By \ref{chV-4-4} and \ref{chV-2-27} we have
        \begin{equation*}
            \{ \text{anodyne extensions} \} = \{ \text{cofibrations }\} \cap \{ \text{weak homotopy equivalences}\}
        \end{equation*}	
        and the latter two are clearly right cancelable against all maps).

        \emph{Proof of \ref{chVI-3-41}}: By assumption, $i \boxstar j \in \Sigma$ is equivalent to admitting left lifts against $J$, i.e. every diagram
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                A \star Y \underset{A \star X}{\cup} B \star X \arrow[d, "i \, \boxstar \, j"'] \arrow[r] & K \arrow[d, "f"] \\
                B \star Y \arrow[ur, dashed] \arrow[r] & L
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}	
        admitting a filler for all $f \in J$. But by adjunction this is the same as
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                A \arrow[d, "i"'] \arrow[r] & K_{/ B \to K} \arrow[d] \\
                B \arrow[ru, dashed] \arrow[r] & (K_{/ A \to K}) \underset{L_{/ A \to L}}{\times} (L_{/ B \to L})
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        admitting fillers, which defines a saturated class by \ref{chV-2-2}.\\
        The other case is analogous using right instead of left slices.\hfill$\Box$

        \emph{Proof of \ref{chVI-3-18}}: Part i) is obvious (and we used it in \ref{chVI-3-30} already). For part ii) consider the class
        \begin{equation*}
            C = \{ k \in \mor (\sSet) \mid k \boxstar l \text{ is inner anodyne for all cofibrations } l\}
        \end{equation*}	
        By \ref{chVI-3-34} it is an intersection of saturated classes and thus itself saturated. We need to show that it contains the right anodynes, so it suffices to show
        \begin{equation*}
            \horn{n}{i} \to \Delta^n \in C
        \end{equation*}
        for all $0 < i \leq n$. But consider
        \begin{equation*}
            D = \{ l \in \mor (\sSet) \mid (\horn{n}{i} \to \Delta^n) \boxstar l \text{ is inner anodyne}\}
        \end{equation*}
        Again, this is saturated by \ref{chVI-3-34}, and we have to show that all cofibrations are contained in $D$, for which it suffices to treat the boundary inclusions $\partial \Delta^k \to \Delta^k$ by exercise 2, sheet 7. But lemma \ref{chVI-3-30} identifies $(\horn{n}{i} \to \Delta^n) \boxstar (\partial \Delta^k \to \Delta^k)$ as an inner horn, which is certainly inner anodyne.\\
        The argument in the other cases is the same.\hfill$\Box$

    \subsection{Proposition}\label{chVI-3-43}
        Let $A \xrightarrow{i} X \xrightarrow{f} Y \xrightarrow{p}$ be composable morphisms maps of simplicial sets, such that $i$ is a cofibration and $p$ as inner fibration, Then consider the maps
        \begin{equation*}
            s \colon Y_{/ f} \to Y_{/ f i} \underset{B_{/ p f i}}{\times} B_{/ p f}
        \end{equation*}
        and
        \begin{equation*}
            t \colon Y_{f /} \to Y_{f i /} \underset{B_{p f i /}}{\times} B_{p f /} \,. 
        \end{equation*}
        \begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*)]
            \item 
                $t$ is a left fibration and $s$ a right fibration
            \item 
                if $p$ is a right fibration, then $t$ is a Kan fibration, and if $p$ is a left fibration, then $s$ is a Kan fibration
            \item 
                if $p$ is a trivial fibration, then so are $t$ and $s$
            \item 
                if $i$ is right anodyne, then $t$ is a trivial fibration, and if $i$ is left anodyne then $s$ is a trivial fibration.
        \end{enumerate}

        \emph{Proof}: We prove statements about $t$: As discussed, a lifting problem
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                S \arrow[d, "a"'] \arrow[r] & Y_{f /} \arrow[d, "t"] \\
                T \arrow[ru, dashed] \arrow[r] & Y_{f i /} \underset{B_{p f i /}}{\times} B_{p f /}
            \end{tikzcd}	
        \end{equation*}
        is equivalent to
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                A \star T \underset{A \star S}{\cup} X \star S \arrow[d, "i \, \boxstar \, a"'] \arrow[r] & Y \arrow[d, "p"] \\
                X \star T \arrow[ru, dashed] \arrow[r] & B
            \end{tikzcd}	
        \end{equation*}
        \begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*)]
            \item 
                To show that $t$ is a left fibration we consider the inclusions $\horn{n}{i} \to \Delta^n$ for $0 \leq i < n$ for $a$, which are left anodyne by definition. Thus \ref{chVI-3-18} shows that $i \boxstar a$ is then inner anodyne, so since $p$ is an inner fibration, the lower diagram admits a lift.
            \item 
                If we take $a = \horn{n}{n} \to \Delta^n$ then \ref{chVI-3-18} gives that $i \boxstar a$ is still right anodyne so a lift exists whenever $p$ is a right fibration.
            \item 
                If we take $a = \partial \Delta^n \to \Delta^n$ then $i \boxstar a$ is still a cofibration, so a lift exists if $p$ is a trivial fibration.
            \item 
                If again $a = \partial \Delta^n \to \Delta^n$, but $i$ is right anodyne, then $i \boxstar a$ is inner anodyne by \ref{chVI-3-18}, so a lift always exists.\hfill$\Box$
        \end{enumerate}

    \subsection{Corollary}\label{chVI-3-44}
        If $f \colon \Ii \to \Cc$ is a map to a quasicategory then
        \begin{equation*}
            s \colon \Cc_{/ f} \to \Cc \qquad t \colon \Cc_{f /} \to \Cc
        \end{equation*}	
        are a right and left fibration, respectively.\\
        In particular, $\Cc_{/ f}$ and $\Cc_{f /}$ are again quasicategories.
        \medskip

        Before we finish the proof of Joyal's theorem, a remark:

    \subsection{Remark}\label{chVI-3-45}
        We now have two competing definitions for over- and under-categories of quasicategories: On the one hand for $X \colon \Delta^0 \to \Cc$ there are the categories
        \begin{equation*}
            \Cc_{/X} \quad \text{and} \quad \Cc_{X /}
        \end{equation*}
        defined using joins and on the other hand there are
        \begin{equation*}
            \Cc \big/ X \quad \text{and} \quad X \big/ \Cc
        \end{equation*}
        defined using the arrow category. Worse yet, the latter construction can be extended as follows: Given $F \colon \Ii \to \Cc$ and $G \Jj \to \Cc$ put
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                F \big/ G \arrow[d] \arrow[r] \arrow[rd, phantom, "\lrcorner", very near end] & \Ar(\Cc) \arrow[d, "(s{,}t)"] \\
                \Ii \times \Jj \arrow[r] & \Cc \times \Cc
            \end{tikzcd}	
        \end{equation*}
        As $(s, t)$ is an inner fibration $f \big/ G$ is a quasicategory whenever $\Ii$ and $\Jj$ are such, and it follows from \ref{chVI-3-31} that the map $f \big/ G \to \Ii \times \Jj$ is a left fibration if $\Ii \simeq *$ and a right fibration if $\Jj \simeq *$. But then 
        \begin{equation*}
            \Cc_{\sslash f} \coloneq \const \colon \Cc \to \Fun(\Ii, \Cc) \Big/ f \colon \Delta^0 \to \Fun(\Ii, \Cc)
        \end{equation*}
        is another perfectly reasonable attempt at defining a category of cones over a functor $f \colon \Ii \to \Cc$.\\
        It will be  an exercise to show, that $\Cc_{\sslash f}$ and $\Cc_{/ f}$ agree for nerves of ordinary categories, but this is not true in general. Furthermore, since by definition both
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \Hom_{\Cc}(Y, X) \arrow[d] \arrow[r] & \Cc \big/ X \arrow[d] \\
                \Delta^0 \arrow[r, "Y"] & \Cc
            \end{tikzcd}
            \quad
            \text{and}
            \quad
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \Hom_{\Cc}(Y, X) \arrow[d] \arrow[r] & Y \big/ \Cc \arrow[d] \\
                \Delta^0 \arrow[r, "X"] & \Cc
            \end{tikzcd}	
        \end{equation*}
        are pullbacks, the diagrams
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \Hom_{\Cc}^R(Y, X) \arrow[d] \arrow[r] \arrow[rd, phantom, "\lrcorner", very near end] & \Cc_{/ X} \arrow[d] \\
                \Delta^0 \arrow[r, "Y"] & \Cc
            \end{tikzcd}
            \quad
            \text{and}
            \quad
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \Hom_{\Cc}^L(Y, X) \arrow[d] \arrow[r] \arrow[rd, phantom, "\lrcorner", very near end] & \Cc_{Y /} \arrow[d] \\
                \Delta^0 \arrow[r, "X"] & \Cc
            \end{tikzcd}	
        \end{equation*}
        give reasonable definitions of mapping spaces (they are Kan by \ref{chVI-3-44} and \ref{chVI-3-27}). As the notation suggests these are different from $\Hom_{\Cc}(Y, X)$ and inn fact from one another! There are natural injections
        \begin{equation*}
            \Hom_{\Cc}^R(Y, X) \hookrightarrow \Hom_{\Cc}(Y, X) \hookleftarrow \Hom_{\Cc}^L(Y, X)
        \end{equation*}
        but it is a non-trivial result that these are homotopy equivalences, and similarly, that the two categories of cones are in fact equivalent.\\
        The formation of fat slice categories also admits a left adjoint, the \emph{fat join}, so one may wonder, why bother with the usual join and slices at all... The answer is that necessarily the fat join of $\Delta^n$ and $\Delta^m$ is \emph{not} $\Delta^{n+m+1}$, so the one thing they cannot obviously be used for is a proof of Joyal's lifting theorem! Quite the pickle...

        These issues will come facts to haunt us when establishing Theorem H, as $\Hom_{\Cc}(X, Y)$ is more easily compared to $\Hom_{\Nn^c(\Cc)}^L(X, Y)$ than $\Hom_{\Nn^c(\Cc)}(X, Y)$.
        \medskip

        We are finally ready to prove Joyal's theorem:

    \subsection{Proof of \ref{chvI-3-20} (existence)}
        By \ref{chVI-3-35} the lifting problem
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \horn{n}{0} \arrow[d] \arrow[r, "f"] & \Cc \arrow[d, "F"] \\
                \Delta^n \arrow[ru, dashed] \arrow[r] & \Dd
            \end{tikzcd}	
        \end{equation*}	
        is equivalent to
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \Delta^0 \arrow[d, "d_1"'] \arrow[r] & \Cc_{/ \Delta^{n-2}} \arrow[d] \\
                \Delta^1 \arrow[ru, dashed] \arrow[r] & \Cc_{/ \partial \Delta^{n-2}} \underset{\Dd_{\partial \Delta^{n-2}}}{\times} \Dd_{/ \Delta^{n-2}}
            \end{tikzcd}	
        \end{equation*}
        As $F$ is an inner fibration and $\partial \Delta^{n-2} \to \Delta^{n-2}$ a cofibration the right hand vertical map is a right fibration by \ref{chVI-3-43}. Furthermore, by construction there is a commutative diagram
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \Delta^1 \arrow[dd, "(01)"'] \arrow[r] & \Cc_{/ \partial \Delta^{n-2}} \underset{\Dd_{\partial \Delta^{n-2}}}{\times} \Dd_{/ \Delta^{n-2}} \arrow[d] \arrow[r] \arrow[rd, phantom, "\ulcorner" very near end] & \Dd_{/ \Delta^{n-2}} \arrow[d, color=magenta] \\
                & \Cc_{/ \partial \Delta^{n-2}} \arrow[d, color=magenta] \arrow[r] & \Dd_{/ \partial \Delta^{n-2}} \\
                \horn{n}{0} \arrow[r, "f"] & \Cc &
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        By \ref{chVI-3-43} these two magenta vertical maps are right fibrations, thus so is the middle vertical composite. This implies first that $\Cc_{/ \partial \Delta^{n-2}} \underset{\Dd_{\partial \Delta^{n-2}}}{\times} \Dd_{/ \Delta^{n-2}}$ is a quasicategory and secondly \ref{chV-38} implies that
        \begin{equation*}
            \Delta^1 \to \Cc_{/ \partial \Delta^{n-2}} \underset{\Dd_{\partial \Delta^{n-2}}}{\times} \Dd_{/ \Delta^{n-2}}
        \end{equation*}
        represents an equivalence as the composite
        \begin{equation*}
            \Delta^1 \to \horn{n}{0} \xrightarrow{f} \Cc
        \end{equation*}
        does so by assumption. But then \ref{chVI-3-36} and \ref{chVI-3-38} give the desired lift.\hfill$\Box$
        \medskip

        To establish uniqueness of the lift in Joyal's theorem it suffices to show:

    \subsection{Lemma}\label{chVI-3-46}
        If $p \colon \Cc \to \Dd$ is a right fibration between quasicategories, then the fibre of the map
        \begin{equation*}
            r \,\colon \Fun(\Delta^1, \Cc) \to \Fun(\Delta^1, \Dd)_s \underset{\Dd}{\times} \Cc
        \end{equation*}
        over a pair $(f, x)$ is a contractible Kan complex, whenever $f \colon \Delta^1 \to \Dd$ is an equivalence.
        \medskip

        The proof will use \ref{chVI-3-27}, which in turn relies only on the existence part of \ref{chVI-3-20}, so we avoid circularity.
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}: Since $p$ is conservative by \ref{chVI-3-28}, the diagram
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \core(\Cc) \arrow[d, "\core(p)"'] \arrow[r] & \Cc \arrow[d, "p"] \\
                \core(\Dd) \arrow[r] & \Dd
            \end{tikzcd}	
        \end{equation*}
        is a pullback. In particular, $\core(p)$ is again a right fibration, and thus a Kan fibration by \ref{chVI-3-27}. But then by \ref{chV-2-26} the map
        \begin{equation*}
            \overline{r} \,\colon \Fun(\Delta^1, \core(\Cc)) \to \Fun(\Delta^1, \core(\Dd)) \underset{\Fun(\{0\}, \core(\Dd))}{\times} \Fun(\{0\}, \core(\Cc))
        \end{equation*}
        is a trivial fibration, so in particular has contractible fibres. It remains to check that
        \begin{equation*}
            \overline{r}^{-1}(f, x) = r^{-1}(f, x)
        \end{equation*}
        whenever $f$ is an equivalence. But these simplicial sets have the same vertices and edges as $p$ is conservative. This is immediate for the vertices and for edges one decodes that an edge of $r^{-1}(f, x)$ is a diagram $\Delta^1 \times \Delta^1 \to \Cc$:
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzpicture}[
                scale=0.9, 
                thick, 
                color=violet,

    \subsection{Proof of \ref{chVI-3-20} (uniqueness)}
        Apply \ref{chVI-3-46} to the reformulation of the lifting problem in the proof of existence.\hfill$\Box$
        \medskip

        Finally, we deduce the formulation of Joyal's theorem given in \ref{chVI-3-16}. Given a morphism $f \colon x \to y$ in a quasicategory $\Cc$ consider the functors
        \begin{equation*}
            \Cc_{/ x} \leftarrow \Cc_{/ f} \rightarrow \Cc_{/ y} 
        \end{equation*}
        arising from the sequences $\Delta^0 \xrightarrow{0 \text{ or } 1} \Delta^1 \xrightarrow{f} \Cc \rightarrow *$. Then by \ref{chVI-3-43} the left functor is a trivial Kan fibration, so in particular an equivalence of categories by \ref{chVI-2-12}. Choosing an inverse (of which there is a contractible choice by \ref{chVI-3-24}) we obtain a functor
        \begin{equation*}
            f_* \,\colon \Cc_{/ x} \to \Cc_{/ y}
        \end{equation*}
        that on vertices is given by postcomposition with $f$. Analogously, there is a functor
        \begin{equation*}
            f^* \,\colon \Cc_{y /} \to \Cc_{x /}
        \end{equation*}
        given by precomposition with $f$. We then have the following refinement of \ref{chVI-3-20}:

    \subsection{Proposition}\label{chVI-3-47}
        For a morphism $f \colon x \to y$ in a quasicategory $\Cc$ the statements
        \begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*)]
            \item 
                $f$ is an equivalence \label{chVI-3-47-i}
            \item 
                $\Cc_{/ f} \to \Cc_{/ y}$ is a trivial Kan fibration \label{chVI-3-47-ii}
            \item 
                $\Cc_{/ f} \to \Cc_{/ y}$ is an equivalence of categories \label{chVI-3-47-iii}
            \item 
                'the' functor $f_* \colon \Cc_{/ x} \to \Cc_{/ y}$ is an equivalence of categories \label{chVI-3-47-iv}
        \end{enumerate}	
        are equivalent.
        \smallskip

        The analogous statements using under-categories hold as well.
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}: $\ref{chVI-3-47-i} \to \ref{chVI-3-47-ii}$: A lifting problem
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \partial \Delta^n \arrow[d] \arrow[r] & \Cc_{/ f} \arrow[d] \\
                \Delta^n \arrow[r] & \Cc_{/ x}
            \end{tikzcd}	
        \end{equation*}
        by using \ref{chVI-3-35} translates to
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \Delta^1 \arrow[r] \arrow[rr, "f", bend left] & \horn{n+2}{0} \arrow[d] \arrow[r] & \Cc \arrow[d] \\
                & \Delta^{n+2} \arrow[r] & * 
            \end{tikzcd}	
        \end{equation*}
        which has a solution by \ref{chVI-3-20} if $f$ is an equivalence. \\
        $\ref{chVI-3-47-ii} \to \ref{chVI-3-47-iii}$: \ref{chVI-2-12}. \\
        $\ref{chVI-3-47-iii} \to \ref{chVI-3-47-iv}$: Immediate from the homotopy commutative diagram
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \Cc_{/ x} \arrow[rd, "f_*"] & \Cc_{/ f} \arrow[l] \arrow[d] \\
                & \Cc_{/ y}
            \end{tikzcd}	
        \end{equation*}	
        since the horizontal map is an equivalence.
        $\ref{chVI-3-47-iv} \to \ref{chVI-3-47-i}$: If $f_* \colon \Cc_{/ x} \to \Cc_{/ y}$ is an equivalence of categories we can in particular pick a $g \in \Cc_{/ x}$ such that $f_* g$ is equivalent to $\id_y$ in $\Cc_{/ y}$. But decoding this means that there is a $2$-simplex in $\Cc$
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzpicture}[
                scale=0.9, 
                thick, 
                color=violet, 
                every node/.style={text=black}
            ]
                    \coordinate (A) at (0, 0);
                    \coordinate (B) at (1, 1.73);
                    \coordinate (C) at (2, 0);

                    \node[below] at (A) {$y$};
                    \node[below] at (C) {$y$};                
                    \draw[postaction={nomorepostaction,decorate,decoration={markings,mark=at position 0.6 with {\arrow{>[length=8pt,width=5pt]}}}}] (A) -- node[left] {$s$} (B);
                    \draw[postaction={nomorepostaction,decorate,decoration={markings,mark=at position 0.6 with {\arrow{>[length=8pt,width=5pt]}}}}] (B) -- node[right] {$h$} (C);
                    \draw[postaction={nomorepostaction,decorate,decoration={markings,mark=at position 0.6 with {\arrow{>[length=8pt,width=5pt]}}}}] (A) -- node[below] {$\id_y$} (C);
                    
            \end{tikzpicture}        
        \end{equation*}	
        where $s$ is an equivalence and $h$ a composite of $f$ and $g$. But this means that $\id_y$ is a composite of $f$ and $g s$. So $f$ admits a right inverse. But then the images of $\id_x$ and $g s f$ in $\Cc_{/ y}$ are equivalent since
        \begin{equation*}
            f_* \id_x \sim f \sim \id_y f \sim f g s f \sim f_* g s f \,.
        \end{equation*}
        But as $f_*$ is an equivalence this implies that $\id_y$ and $g s f$ are equivalent in $\Cc_{/ x}$. Decoding again, we find this implies that $g s f$ is an equivalence, so $f$ also admits a left inverse, hence is an equivalence.\hfill$\Box$

    \subsection{Remark}\label{chVI-3-48}
        We have \emph{not} constructed a functor
        \begin{equation*}
            \Cat_{\infty \Cc_{/}} \to \Cat_{\infty}, \quad f \colon \Cc \to \Dd \mapsto \Dd_{f /} \text{ or } \Dd_{/ f}
        \end{equation*}
        or anything similar so far! This is quite difficult, in fact already showing that an equivalence of categories $g \colon \Dd \to \Dd'$ gives equivalences
        \begin{equation*}
            \Dd_{f /} \to \Dd'_{g f /} \quad \text{and} \quad \Dd_{/ f} \to \Dd'_{/ g f}
        \end{equation*}
        is a highly non-trivial endeavor. Here again the fat slices are better behaved; the latter statement is almost a triviality here. To obtain a functor one 'only' needs to understand the interaction of the coherent nerve functor with over- and under-categories to build a simplicial model for $\Cat_{\infty \Cc_{/}}$; alternatively the statement will follow from general machinery about limits in quasicategories.

        In fact the statement about thin slices is usually proved by reduction to the corresponding one for fat slices!
