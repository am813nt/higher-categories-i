\chapter{Quasi-categories}

    We shall here develop just the language of quasi-categories just sufficiently to state the main results of the lecture course. The proofs are still very much out of reach.
    
    We start off with some terminology: if $\Cc$ is a quasi-category than we shall refer to $\Cc_0$ as the objects of $\Cc$ and to $\Cc_1$ as its morphisms. The later will be denoted $f \colon x \to y$ if $d_0f = y$ and $d_1f = x$. The degenerate $1$-simplex with boundaries $x$ will be referred to as $\id_x$.
    
    In distinction with ordinary categories, however, quasi-categories have an entire simplicial set of morphisms between two objects:

    \section{Definition}
        Let $x, y \in \Cc$ be objects. We define $\Hom_{\Cc}(x, y) \in \sSet$ by
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \Hom_{\Cc}(x, y) \arrow[r] \arrow[d]
                \arrow[dr, phantom, "\pb", very near end, end anchor={[xshift=-1ex]north}]
                & F(\Delta^1, \Cc) \arrow{d}{(d_1^*, d_0^*)} \\
                \Delta^0 \arrow{r}{(x,y)}
                & \Cc \times \Cc
            \end{tikzcd}        
        \end{equation*}

    \section{Lemma}
        For $x, y \in \Cc$ we have a canonical isomorphism
        \begin{equation*}
            \pi_0 \Hom_{\Cc}(x, y) \cong \Hom_{\Cc}(x, y) \,.
        \end{equation*}
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}. On the one hand, $\pi_0 \Hom_{\Cc}(x, y)$ is given by
        \begin{equation*}
            \left\{ f \in \Cc_1 \mid d_0 f = y, d_1 f = x \right\} / {\sim}
        \end{equation*}
        where $\sim$ is generated by $d_0 H \sim d_1 H$ for every $H \in \Hom_{\Cc}(x, y)_1$. But decoding, this means $H \colon \Delta^1 \times \Delta^1 \to \Cc$:
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzpicture}[
                scale=0.8, 
                thick, 
                color=violet, 
                every node/.style={text=black}
            ]

                % Define Coordinates
                \coordinate (TL) at (0,2); % Top Left
                \coordinate (TR) at (2,2); % Top Right
                \coordinate (BL) at (0,0); % Bottom Left
                \coordinate (BR) at (2,0); % Bottom Right

                % Draw Square Edges
                \draw (TL) -- node[above] {$d_0 H$} (TR);
                \draw (BL) -- node[below] {$d_1 H$} (BR);
    
                % Draw Vertical Edges with rotated labels
                % 'rotate=90' aligns the text vertically reading upwards
                \draw (BL) -- node[left] {$\id_x$} (TL);
                \draw (BR) -- node[right] {$\id_y$} (TR);

                % Draw Diagonal
                % 'sloped' aligns text with the line
                \draw (TL) -- node[sloped, below] {} (BR);

            \end{tikzpicture}          
        \end{equation*}
        and we saw in \ref{chII-30} that this is precisely the relation imposed on morphisms in $\pi \Cc$. Since \ref{chII-31} $\pi \Cc \cong h \Cc$, the claim follows.\hfill$\Box$
        \medskip

        Furthermore, the conflict of notation is minimal:

        \section{Lemma}
        For $\Cc$ an ordinary small category and $x, y \in \Cc$ we have a canonical isomorphism
        \begin{equation*}
            \Hom_{\Nn \Cc}(x, y) \cong \mathbb{D} \Hom_{\Cc}(x, y) \,.
        \end{equation*}

        \emph{Proof}. Exercise.
        \medskip

        Here is the first result we do not prove yet:

        \section{Theorem A}\label{chIII-4}
        For any $x, y \in \Cc$ the simplicial set $\Hom_{\Cc}(x, y)$ is Kan.
        \smallskip

        We will derive this from basic properties of left/right fibrations that we will develop soon. In any case, this is the sense in which a quasi-category has a space of morphisms between any two objects.

        Next we turn to composition: For morphisms $g \colon x \to y$ and $f \colon y \to z$ in $\Cc$ we say that $h \colon x \to z$ is a composition of $f$ and $g$ if $\left[ f \right] \circ \left[ g \right] = \left[ h \right]$ in $h\Cc$. Again this notion is underlain simplicial one:

        \section{Definition}\label{chIII-5}
        Given $g \colon x \to y$ and $f \colon y \to z$ in $\Cc$ we define $\comp(f, g) \in \sSet$ by
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{tikzcd}
                \comp(f, g) \arrow[r] \arrow[d] \arrow[dr, phantom, "\pb", very near end, end anchor={[xshift=-1ex]north}] & F(\Delta^2, \Cc) \arrow[d] \\
                \Delta^0 \arrow[r, "{(f,\, g)}"] & F(\horn{2}{1}, \Cc)
            \end{tikzcd}
        \end{equation*}
        \emph{Warning}: $\comp(f, g)_0 \nsubseteq \Cc_1$
        \smallskip

        It will be another exercise to show, that $\pi_0 \comp(f, g) = *$.
        \medskip

        Here is another theorem to be proven later:

        \section{Theorem B}\label{chIII-6}
        For any $f \colon y \to z$ and $g \colon x \to z$ in $\Cc$ we have that $\comp(f, g)$ is a contractible Kan set.
        \smallskip

        Again this follows from the theory of Kan fibrations and anodyne extensions, but is not easy to see directly. The condition that $\comp(f, g)$ be as above is almost equivalent to being a quasi-category; I had used that in the introduction to motivate the definition.

        For an ordinary category $\Cc$ the right map in \ref{chIII-5} is an isomorphism (though this is not entirely trivial either) so $\comp(f, g) \simeq *$ in that case.

        \section{Definition}
        A morphism $f \colon x \to y$ in $\Cc$ is called an \emph{equivalence} if there exists a morphism $g \colon y \to x$ such that $\id_x$ is a composition of $f$ and $g$ and $\id_y$ one of $g$ and $f$.

        \section{Lemma}
        A morphism $f \colon x \to y$ in $\Cc$ is an equivalence if and only if $\left[ f \right] \colon x \to y$ in $hC$ is an isomorphism.
        \smallskip

        \emph{Proof}. The 'if'-direction follows directly from exercise 3 of sheet 4 and $h\Cc \cong \pi \Cc$, while the 'only if'-direction is trivial.\hfill$\Box$

        \section{Definition}
        A quasi-category in which every morphism is an equivalence is called an \emph{anima}, or quasi-groupoid or $\infty$-groupoid.
        \smallskip

        So $\Cc$ is a anima if and only if $h\Cc$ is a groupoid. The new term anima reflects that these objects are fundamental to the theory of higher categories as sets are to ordinary categories, because of the following (together with \hyperref[chIII-4]{Theorem A}):

        \section{Proposition}
        Any Kan set is an anima.
        \smallskip

        The converse is much more difficult:

        \section{Theorem C (Joyal)}
        Any anima is a Kan set.
        \smallskip

        And while we can define individual compositions, the construction of a composition map
        \begin{equation*}
            \Hom_{\Cc}(y, z) \times \Hom_{\Cc}(x, y) \to \Hom_{\Cc}(x, z)
        \end{equation*}
        is beyond our current means. Its existence will follow from the result underlying \hyperref[chIII-6]{Theorem B}. Here is the connection: The left hand side identifies with a simplicial-sub-set of $F(\horn{2}{1})$, which we will show equivalent to $F(\Delta^2, \Cc)$ under restriction. Composing an inverse of that equivalence with
        \begin{equation*}
            d_1^* \,\colon F(\Delta^2, \Cc) \to F(\Delta^1, \Cc)
        \end{equation*}
        will then give the desired map, since $\Hom_{\Cc}(x, z) \subseteq F(\Delta^1, \Cc)$.
        \medskip

        Next up, functors! A functor between quasi-categories is nothing but a map of simplicial sets $\Cc \to  \Dd$ and a natural transformation is nothing but a homotopy of simplicial map. In fact:

        \section{Theorem D}\label{chIII-12}
        The simplicial set $F(\Cc, \Dd)$ is again a quasi-category and an anima if $\Dd$ is one.
        \smallskip

        In particular, we will denote $F(\Cc, \Dd)$ by $\Fun(\Cc, \Dd)$, the \emph{category of functors}, whenever $\Cc$ and $\Dd$ are quasi-categories. From the above we, in particular obtain notions of \emph{natural equivalence}, i.e. an equivalence in $\Fun(\Cc, \Dd)$, and \emph{equivalence of quasi-categories}, i.e. functors $\Cc \to \Dd$ and $\Dd \to \Cc$ whose composites are naturally equivalent to the identities of $\Cc$ and $\Dd$, respectively. One of the last things we (hopefully) will show is:

        \section{Theorem E}
        A natural transformation $\eta \colon F \Rightarrow G$ is a natural equivalence of functors $\Cc \to \Dd$, if and only of for every $x \in \Cc$, the morphism
        \begin{equation*}
            \eta_x \,\colon F(x) \to G(x)
        \end{equation*}
        in $\Dd$ is an equivalence.

        \section{Theorem F}
        A functor $F \colon \Cc \to \Dd$ is an equivalence of quasi-categories, if and only if it induces an equivalence of categories $h\Cc \to h\Dd$ and for every $x, y \in \Cc$ a homotopy equivalence
        \begin{equation*}
            \Hom_{\Cc}(x, y) \to \Hom_{\Dd}(F(x), F(y)) \,.
        \end{equation*}

        Clearly, there should also be (non-small) quasi-categories of (small) quasi-categories and (small) anima, to be called $\Cat_{\infty}$ and $\An$. Denoting the full subcategories of $\sSet$ spanned by the anima and quasi-categories by $\Kan \subseteq \qCat \subseteq \sSet$, there should be inclusions
        \begin{equation*}
            \Nn \Kan \subseteq \An \quad \text{and} \quad \Nn \qCat \subseteq \Cat_{\infty}
        \end{equation*}
        which are equalities on $0$- and $1$-simplices, but the right hand side will be much bigger. The difference is best illustrated by $2$-simplices in $\Nn \Kan$ and $\An$.         Our ultimate goal will be to analyse the quasi-category $\An$ enough to prove \emph{Grothendieck's homotopy hypothesis}:

        \section{Theorem G (Cordier-Joyal)}
        The quasi-category $\An$ is equivalent to the quasi-category of cell complexes. In particular, $\Hom_{\An}(X, Y)$ is equivalent to the simplicial set (in fact, Kan set) of maps $\gr{X} \to \gr{Y}$, for all anima $X, Y$.
        \medskip

        Here is a sample of a theorem we will not get close to showing this semester:
        \smallskip

        \emph{Theorem (Lurie)}
        For every quasi-category $\Cc$, there exists a functor
        \begin{equation*}
            \Hom_{\Cc} \,\colon \Cc^{\op} \times \Cc \to \An
        \end{equation*}
        extending the anima $\Hom_{\Cc}$ defined earlier.
        \smallskip

        Note that such a functor is required to even share a version of Yoneda's Lemma, the necessity of which I hope to have convinced you of.
